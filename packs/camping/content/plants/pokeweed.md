# Pokeweed

## A confusing plant
Pokeweed is famous in Southern US foraging traditions ("poke salad," "poke sallet") as a cooked edible — but only the young spring shoots, and only after multiple boilings with water changes. Adult plants, roots, mature stems, leaves past about 8 inches, berries, and seeds are all toxic. People are poisoned regularly by eating the wrong part of the wrong-stage plant or by trying it raw. As with all wild plants, this app will not identify any part as safe to eat.

## Identification
A robust perennial herb that grows 4–10 feet tall in a single season. Smooth purplish-red stems thickening to nearly an inch at the base. Large oval leaves with smooth edges, 6–12 inches long. Long drooping clusters of small white-green flowers, ripening to dark purple-black juicy berries on red-purple stems. Found across the eastern US and into the Midwest, in disturbed soil, fencerows, edges of fields, gardens, and forest openings.

## What's toxic about it
The toxin (phytolaccin and related compounds) causes severe gastrointestinal symptoms and can affect the heart and nervous system. Toxicity is highest in the root and mature parts; lowest in young spring shoots. Berries are particularly dangerous to children — they look like blackberries or blueberries and have a juicy sweet appearance. Birds eat them without harm and spread the seeds.

## Symptoms
Severe burning in mouth and throat, intense vomiting and watery diarrhea (often blood-tinged), abdominal cramps, weakness, low blood pressure, sometimes confusion or seizures. Onset is 2–6 hours after eating. In severe cases (especially in children who ate berries), cardiovascular collapse can be fatal. Most adult ingestions recover with supportive care within 24–48 hours.

## What to do
Call Poison Control. Evacuate to medical care for any significant ingestion, especially in children. Treatment is supportive: IV fluids to replace what's lost in vomiting and diarrhea, antiemetics, electrolyte monitoring. There is no antidote. Activated charcoal can help if given early. Do not induce vomiting at home unless directed.

## Pokeweed berry stains
The juice is intensely purple — pokeweed berries have been used as ink and dye for centuries. Hands and clothing stained by berries should be washed thoroughly, but skin contact isn't typically toxic. Don't let children play with the berries; the juice in eyes causes irritation and the berries are tempting to taste.

## Tradition vs safety
Generations of Southerners have eaten "poke salad" safely by harvesting only young spring shoots (under 6 inches) and boiling them three times in fresh water, discarding the water each time. This is a real tradition with real food. It is also a tradition that includes occasional poisonings when the wrong part is harvested or the boiling step is skipped. This pack will not guide on the technique because the failure modes are too dangerous. If you want to learn, find a Southern forager in person.
