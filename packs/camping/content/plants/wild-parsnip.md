# Wild Parsnip

## Why it matters
Wild parsnip is invasive across the upper Midwest, Northeast, and parts of the Pacific Northwest. The plant's sap contains furanocoumarins — chemicals that, when activated by sunlight on skin, cause severe burns and blisters that look like second-degree thermal burns. The reaction is the same chemistry as giant hogweed (see plants.giant-hogweed) but somewhat less severe. Workers clearing brush in infested areas get hospitalized regularly.

## Identification
Tall plant, 2–5 feet, with yellow umbrella-shaped flower clusters in mid-summer (June–July). Stems are hollow, grooved, branched. Leaves pinnately compound with toothed leaflets, looking somewhat like celery leaves. Roots are long, white, smell like parsnip — they're the same species as cultivated parsnip; just the wild version is small, woody, and accompanied by toxic above-ground parts. Common along roadsides, in old fields, prairie remnants, and disturbed soil.

## How the burns happen
You contact the sap (brushing through a stand, weed-whacking, cutting). The clear sap dries on skin. Within 24–48 hours, sunlight (UV) on the contaminated skin triggers a chemical reaction in the skin cells — phytophotodermatitis. Red painful blistering appears in the pattern of contact: streaks where stems brushed legs, splatter patterns on arms. Pain can be severe; blisters can be deep. The affected skin remains photosensitive for weeks to months, sunburning dramatically.

## If you contact the sap
Wash thoroughly with soap and cold water as soon as possible. Keep the contacted area out of sun for at least 48 hours — cover with long sleeves, long pants, full coverage. The reaction is sun-activated; if you can prevent UV exposure, you may prevent the burn entirely. Once blistering develops, treat like thermal burns: cool the area, cover with sterile non-stick dressing, do not pop blisters. Severe burns or large areas need medical care.

## Long-term sun sensitivity
Even after the initial blisters heal, the affected skin remains hyper-sensitive to sunburn for months. The healed pattern can re-darken with sun exposure. Cover affected areas with sunscreen and clothing for at least one full summer after the initial burn. Some people develop permanent dark scars or patches of hyperpigmentation.

## Avoiding it
Long pants, long sleeves, gloves when in known wild parsnip areas. Don't pull or cut wild parsnip without full coverage. Don't weed-whack it — the sap aerosolizes onto skin. If you must remove it (it's a problem invasive in many states), wear chemical-resistant gloves, sleeves to wrists, eye protection, and do the work on a cool overcast day so any sap on skin can be washed off before sun activates it.

## Confusion with cow parsnip and giant hogweed
Wild parsnip has yellow flowers; cow parsnip and giant hogweed both have white flowers. All three are phototoxic. Giant hogweed is the most severe and aggressively invasive. Cow parsnip is native and a milder version of the same reaction (still avoid contact). Knowing each by sight in your region helps you respond appropriately when contact happens.
