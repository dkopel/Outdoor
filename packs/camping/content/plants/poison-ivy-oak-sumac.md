# Poison Ivy, Oak, and Sumac

## Identification
Poison ivy: "leaves of three, let it be." Three leaflets per stem, with the center leaflet on a longer stalk than the two side leaflets. Edges can be smooth or notched but never serrated like blackberry. Reddish in spring, green in summer, yellow/orange/red in fall. Grows as a low ground cover, a shrub, or a climbing vine with hairy aerial roots on tree trunks. Poison oak looks similar but with leaves lobed like oak leaves; common on the West Coast. Poison sumac has clusters of 7–13 leaflets in a feathered arrangement, grows as a small tree or shrub in wet boggy areas of the eastern U.S.

## Why it makes you react
All three plants contain urushiol, an oily resin that triggers a delayed allergic reaction in roughly 85% of people. Reactions appear 12–72 hours after exposure as itchy red streaks, bumps, and oozing blisters in the pattern of contact. The fluid in blisters is not contagious — once washed off skin, urushiol on you can no longer spread the rash, though urushiol on clothing, pets, or gear can transfer to skin for weeks.

## If you contact it
Wash the contacted area with cool water and soap (or any detergent) within 30 minutes if possible. Specialized products like Tecnu work too but plain dish soap is also effective at breaking up the oil. Scrub under fingernails. Wash all clothing and gear that touched the plant in hot water and detergent. Cool baths, calamine lotion, and over-the-counter hydrocortisone cream help with itching once the rash develops. See a doctor if the rash covers a large area, involves the face or genitals, or shows signs of infection (pus, fever, red streaks).

## Burning is dangerous
Never burn poison ivy, oak, or sumac. The urushiol vaporizes into smoke and can be inhaled, causing reactions in the lungs and airways that have killed people. Pull plants by hand only in heavy gloves you can dispose of, and bag the plant material — do not compost. Mowing chops the plant and aerosolizes oil; if you must mow, do it in still air with full coverage of skin and a respirator-grade mask. If you don't need to remove it, the safest action is to leave it alone and avoid the area.
