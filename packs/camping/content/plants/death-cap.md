# Death Cap Mushroom

## The deadliest mushroom in North America
Amanita phalloides is responsible for the vast majority of mushroom poisoning deaths worldwide. Originally European, it has spread across North America, especially the West Coast where it grows under oaks. A single mushroom can kill an adult. Treatment requires hospital intensive care and sometimes liver transplant. Many fatalities are foragers who confused it with the edible paddy straw mushroom or other species.

## Identification
Cap 2–6 inches wide, dome-shaped young, flattening with age. Color is the giveaway: usually pale yellow-green to olive-green, sometimes greenish-bronze, often paler at the edges. Surface smooth, sometimes faintly streaked radially. Gills white, free from the stem (not attached). Stem white, 3–6 inches tall, with a clear ring (annulus) about midway, and a distinctive cup-like sheath (volva) around the base — often buried under leaf litter or soil. White spore print.

## Look-alikes that kill people
Inexperienced foragers mistake it for:
- **Paddy straw mushroom** (Volvariella volvacea, edible) — also has a volva, but pink spore print and pink-ish gills as it matures.
- **Some Russula species** — but Russulas have no ring or volva.
- **Young button mushrooms** — but commercial button mushroom has a pink-to-brown spore print, never white, and never a volva.
- **Caesar's mushroom** (Amanita caesarea, edible in Europe) — yellow-orange cap with yellow gills, NOT pale green.

The volva at the base is the key danger sign. If you ever cup the base of a mushroom and find a sheath or remnants of one — STOP. Half the deadly mushrooms have it.

## Amatoxin: the chemistry of death
Death caps contain amatoxins — heat-stable, water-stable toxins that destroy liver cells. Cooking, drying, freezing, boiling — none reduce toxicity. The toxin is absorbed in the gut, then circulates back through the liver repeatedly, killing more cells each cycle. By the time symptoms appear, significant liver damage has occurred. The first symptoms feel like food poisoning and resolve — this "honeymoon" period is when patients delay seeking care, and it's when they're dying.

## The three-stage timeline
**Stage 1 (6–24 hours after eating):** Sudden severe vomiting, watery diarrhea, abdominal pain. Indistinguishable from gastroenteritis. People dismiss it.
**Stage 2 (24–72 hours):** Apparent recovery. The patient feels much better. Liver and kidney damage is progressing silently.
**Stage 3 (72+ hours):** Jaundice (yellow skin and eyes), bleeding (gums, easy bruising), confusion, coma. Without intensive treatment — often including liver transplant — death typically occurs in 6–16 days.

## What to do
If anyone is suspected of eating ANY part of any unknown mushroom, treat as emergency immediately, regardless of symptoms. Call Poison Control. Get to a hospital that handles mushroom poisoning (academic medical centers in major cities). Bring the remaining mushroom, any vomit, any cooking scraps for identification. Treatments include activated charcoal early (within 1 hour of ingestion), intensive fluid replacement, sometimes silibinin (a milk thistle extract used as antidote in Europe, available in some US hospitals), N-acetylcysteine, and in worst cases liver transplant.

## Prevention
This pack will not identify any mushroom as safe to eat. Even experienced foragers die from misidentifying Amanitas. The only safe approach: do not eat any wild mushroom unless an expert mycologist has identified that specific specimen in person, in your region, and you've eaten the species before with no reaction. Teach children to never put any wild mushroom in their mouth. Watch dogs in oak-forested areas — death caps grow under oaks and dogs eat them.
