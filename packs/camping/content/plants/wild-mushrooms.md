# Wild Mushrooms: Don't Risk It

## Why mushrooms are especially dangerous
There are no universal field rules that distinguish edible from deadly mushrooms. Color, smell, animals eating them, taste tests, silver-spoon tests — every traditional "rule" fails for at least one deadly species. A single Amanita phalloides (death cap) can kill an adult, and it looks similar to several edible species in the genus. Other deadly species (destroying angel, deadly galerina, deadly webcap) are similarly innocuous-looking. Symptoms often appear too late to save the patient. Mushroom identification requires in-person expertise with the specific species you have, plus often microscopic and chemical confirmation.

## What this app won't tell you
This app does not identify wild mushrooms as edible or safe, ever, for any reason, regardless of how confidently you describe the appearance. There is no field guide app or AI assistant capable of safely identifying wild mushrooms. The cost of being wrong is liver failure, kidney failure, or death — not a stomach ache. The only safe rule for outdoor people: do not eat any wild mushroom you found in the field unless an expert mycologist has identified that specific specimen in person.

## Recognize Amanita features
You should know what Amanitas look like so you can avoid the family entirely. Features: a cup-like volva at the base (often hidden under leaf litter — dig gently to expose), a ring (annulus) on the stem, white gills, often white spore print. Cap colors range from white (destroying angel) to green-yellow (death cap) to red (fly agaric) to brown. Several edible species share some Amanita features — which is why even experienced foragers die. If you see ANY of those features, do not eat it, do not handle it carelessly, and wash your hands after touching it.

## Amatoxin poisoning timeline
Death cap and related species contain amatoxins. Symptoms come in stages: 6–24 hours after eating, sudden severe vomiting and watery diarrhea (often mistaken for food poisoning). 24–72 hours, apparent recovery — patient feels better — toxin is destroying the liver invisibly. After 72 hours, liver failure, jaundice, coagulopathy, often kidney failure. Death typically follows in 1–2 weeks without liver transplant. By the time the patient feels sick again, treatment options are limited.

## If suspected ingestion
Call Poison Control immediately (1-800-222-1222 in the US) and get to a hospital that can manage hepatotoxicity — not all small ERs can. Save samples of the mushroom, any vomit, and the mushroom collection bag — these help with identification. Do not wait to see if symptoms develop. Time is critical for some treatment options. Cooking, drying, or freezing do not destroy amatoxins; the heat-stable toxin is what makes amanitas so dangerous.

## Learning safely
If you want to learn mushrooms, the only safe path is in-person apprenticeship with a regional expert over multiple seasons. Local mycological societies offer guided forays. Identification books are useful for confirming an ID you already strongly suspect, never as the primary identifier. Even after years of study, the safest practice is to eat only species with no deadly look-alikes (chanterelles, morels, hen-of-the-woods are common starter species, and even those have look-alikes that cause illness).
