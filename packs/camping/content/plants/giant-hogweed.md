# Giant Hogweed

## Why it's dangerous
Giant hogweed (Heracleum mantegazzianum) is an invasive species in the northeastern US, Pacific Northwest, and parts of Canada. Its sap contains chemicals (furanocoumarins) that, on contact with skin then exposure to sunlight, cause severe burns and blisters — sometimes third-degree. Sap in the eyes can cause temporary or permanent blindness. The plant is striking and tempting to touch or break, especially for kids.

## Identification
Massive — up to 14 feet tall at maturity. Stems are thick (2–4 inches), hollow, with coarse white hairs and dark red-purple blotches. Leaves are huge (up to 5 feet across), deeply lobed, jagged-edged. Flowers are large white umbrella-shaped clusters up to 2.5 feet across, blooming early to mid-summer. Found along stream banks, in moist disturbed soil, woodland edges. Native cow parsnip looks similar but is smaller (4–7 feet) with smaller flower clusters and rounder leaf lobes.

## If you contact the sap
Wash the contacted skin immediately and thoroughly with soap and cold water. Cover the area to keep it out of sunlight for at least 48 hours — UV light is what activates the phototoxic reaction. Long sleeves and gloves on the affected area; no sun exposure on it for several days. If sap got in eyes, flush with water for 20+ minutes and seek immediate medical attention. Sun exposure on contaminated skin within ~24 hours often produces severe blisters within 1–2 days.

## Treating burns
Once burns develop, treat like thermal burns (see first-aid.burns): cool the area, cover with sterile non-stick dressing, do not pop blisters. Burns can be very deep (third-degree) and may scar permanently. The skin remains photosensitive for months or years — the burned area sunburns dramatically even after the initial wound heals. Long-term sun protection on the affected area is needed. Severe or extensive burns need medical care.

## Avoiding it
Long pants, long sleeves, gloves when working in known-infested areas. Identify before you touch any tall plant with umbrella-shaped flowers. Children should be specifically taught what it looks like. Report giant hogweed to local/state agriculture authorities — it's a regulated invasive species in many states, and they often help with safe removal. Never weed-whack or mow it without full PPE; the sap aerosolizes.

## Similar phototoxic plants
Wild parsnip (Pastinaca sativa), cow parsnip (Heracleum maximum), wild celery, fig leaves, and citrus rinds also contain furanocoumarins and can cause phototoxic reactions, though usually less severe than giant hogweed. Same rule applies: wash off immediately, keep out of sun. Bartenders cutting limes and getting "lime burn" on their hands experience a milder version of the same chemistry. If you contact any of these and then get sun, watch for blistering 1–3 days later.
