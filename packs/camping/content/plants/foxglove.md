# Foxglove

## Why it matters
Foxglove contains digitalis glycosides — the same compounds used as a heart medication, with a vanishingly small margin between therapeutic and lethal. Eating even small amounts of leaves, flowers, or seeds can stop the heart. It's a striking ornamental, grows wild in much of the US (especially Pacific Northwest and Northeast), and is sometimes mistaken for edible plants like comfrey by inexperienced foragers.

## Identification
A tall plant, 2–6 feet, with a single unbranched stem rising from a basal rosette of large fuzzy gray-green leaves. Flowers form a dense one-sided spike of bell-shaped blossoms in shades of purple, pink, white, or yellow, each with darker spots inside. Blooms early to mid-summer. Found in woodland clearings, roadsides, gardens (frequently escaped from cultivation), and disturbed soil. Native to Europe but naturalized across much of North America.

## Symptoms of poisoning
Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain within 30 minutes to 2 hours. Then cardiac symptoms: very slow or irregular heart rate, palpitations, low blood pressure, dizziness, weakness, blurred vision (sometimes with yellow-green halos around lights — a classic digitalis effect). Severe cases progress to cardiac arrest. Children, elderly, and people with existing heart disease are at highest risk.

## Common confusions
Foxglove leaves are sometimes mistaken for comfrey, especially before flowering. Comfrey leaves are also fuzzy and gray-green. Comfrey has been used in herbal medicine; people brewing "comfrey tea" from misidentified foxglove leaves have died. Foxglove first-year plants (rosette only) are particularly easy to misidentify. This is another reason this pack refuses to identify plants for consumption.

## Treatment
Immediate evacuation to hospital. Activated charcoal early after ingestion can reduce absorption (only administered by medical staff). Specific antidote exists (digoxin-specific Fab antibodies, "Digibind") and is highly effective for severe poisoning if available. Field treatment is limited to: keeping the patient calm, lying down, monitoring breathing and pulse, evacuating as fast as possible.

## Avoiding it
Don't plant foxglove if you have small children. Teach kids never to eat any garden flower. In the wild, admire the spectacular flower spikes from a distance and do not pick them — sap on hands can transfer to food. Many foragers who used to harvest "wild comfrey" have stopped because the foxglove confusion is too dangerous. As always, no wild plant is safe to eat based on this app or general resemblance to something edible.
