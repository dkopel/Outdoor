# Yew

## Why it's dangerous
Yew (Taxus) is among the most toxic plants in North America. All parts except the red fleshy aril around the seed contain taxine alkaloids, which cause sudden cardiac arrest with little warning. There is no effective antidote and supportive care is often unsuccessful. Yew kills livestock that browse it in pastures and has caused fatal poisonings of children and adults who tried herbal preparations. Used widely as ornamental hedging across the US.

## Identification
Evergreen shrub or small tree, 5–30 feet. Dark green flat needle-like leaves arranged in two ranks along twigs, about 1 inch long, soft to the touch (unlike spruce). Bark is reddish-brown, flaking in plates. The distinctive feature: berries — bright red, fleshy, cup-shaped, with a single hard seed visible through the open end. Looks deceptively appetizing. Native species (Taxus canadensis, Pacific yew) grow wild in parts of the US; ornamental Japanese and English yew species are far more common in landscaped areas.

## The deceptive berry
The red flesh of the berry (aril) is actually not toxic — birds eat them and disperse seeds. But the seed inside is extremely toxic, and the rest of the plant is. Children eating "those pretty red berries" and swallowing the seeds get a high dose. The toxic dose for an adult is approximately one handful of needles or a small number of crushed seeds — far less than people imagine.

## Symptoms
Often there are no warning symptoms — sudden cardiac arrest can occur 1–3 hours after ingestion. When symptoms appear: weakness, dizziness, slow heart rate, dilated pupils, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, then suddenly collapse. Unlike most cardiac toxins (digitalis, oleander) that cause clear early signs, yew often gives little warning before lethal arrhythmia.

## What to do
Any suspected yew ingestion is an emergency. Call Poison Control immediately. Evacuate to a hospital with cardiac monitoring. Treatment is supportive — activated charcoal early, IV fluids, sometimes pacemaker if heart rate is dangerously slow. Outcomes for significant ingestions are poor; survivors usually had small exposures and got rapid medical care.

## Why this matters in the outdoors
You're unlikely to be tempted by yew in true backcountry — it's much more common in residential and park landscaping. The risk is at trailheads, campgrounds in developed parks, and yards near trail starts. Children and pets are at highest risk. Hikers occasionally encounter native yew species in the Pacific Northwest forests; admire without touching. Never use yew as a roasting stick or to flavor food (some folk traditions did this — the result was death).
