# Devil's Club

## Pacific Northwest's worst bushwhacking obstacle
Devil's club is a tall deciduous shrub covered in long sharp spines on its stems, branches, leaf petioles, and even leaf veins. It dominates wet understory in old-growth forest from Alaska through coastal British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, and northern Idaho. Walking through it is genuinely painful — the spines snap off in skin, cause persistent stinging, and frequently lead to infections. Many backcountry travelers in the PNW have memorable scars from devil's club encounters.

## Identification
A spindly shrub, 4–10 feet tall (sometimes taller), with one or few main stems. Large maple-like palmate leaves up to 16 inches across. Stems, undersides of leaves along the veins, and leaf petioles all covered with sharp yellow-tan spines. Small white-green flowers in upright clusters in early summer, ripening to bright red berries in dense clusters. Smell is faintly aromatic when crushed. Found in shaded, wet, often steep forest understory — drainage bottoms, north slopes, stream banks.

## Why the spines are nasty
Devil's club spines are barbed and brittle. They break off in skin and don't easily come out. They carry plant compounds and skin bacteria that make wounds inflamed and slow to heal. Bushwhacking through dense stands leaves people with dozens of spine fragments that take weeks to work out. Wounds frequently get infected — the spines are essentially mini-dirty needles into the skin.

## Avoiding it
Plan routes that avoid known devil's club country. Stay on trail in PNW old-growth. If you must bushwhack, wear thick pants (canvas, denim, not nylon), thick gloves, and a brimmed hat. Long sleeves. Don't grab branches when scrambling; you'll find spines on the stems. Don't sit on moss-covered logs without checking what's around them.

## If you contact it
Remove visible spines carefully with fine-tipped tweezers, pulling straight out along the angle of entry. Some break off and remain — they typically work their way out over days to weeks. Wash the affected area thoroughly with soap and water. Watch for infection: spreading redness, warmth, pus, fever. Use antibiotic ointment on punctures. See a doctor if puncture wounds become red and infected.

## Traditional and modern uses
Indigenous PNW peoples used devil's club extensively as medicine and ceremonial plant — bark preparations for diabetes, respiratory conditions, and ritual cleansing. Modern research has shown some legitimate active compounds. **Do not harvest devil's club for medicinal use without proper indigenous knowledge or scientific guidance**; the plant is culturally significant and ecologically sensitive, and self-prescribing herbal treatments has its own risks.

## Bears in devil's club
Devil's club berry stands are favorite black bear and grizzly food in late summer and fall. The combination of dense vegetation that limits sight distance plus a feeding bear is a real safety concern. Make noise constantly while walking through PNW devil's club country, especially in berry season (late July–September). Bear spray accessible.
