# Other Dangerous Plants to Recognize

Beyond the poison ivy/oak/sumac family and stinging nettle, North American hikers should be aware of several plants that cause severe injury or death. The single best rule: **do not eat, do not touch, do not burn** any plant you cannot identify with absolute confidence.

## Giant hogweed (Heracleum mantegazzianum) — phytophotodermatitis
An invasive plant spreading through the northeastern and northwestern U.S. Tall (8–14 feet at maturity), with massive umbrella-shaped white flower clusters (up to 2.5 feet across), deeply lobed leaves, and a thick stem with purple blotches and stiff white hairs. The sap contains chemicals (furanocoumarins) that, on contact with skin **plus sunlight**, cause severe burns and blistering — often delayed by 24 hours. Permanent scarring is possible. If you contact the sap: wash with cold soapy water, cover the area from sunlight, and seek medical care. Wild parsnip (*Pastinaca sativa*) and cow parsnip cause similar reactions, though usually milder.

## Water hemlock (Cicuta spp.) — most toxic plant in North America
A small white-flowered plant of wet meadows, streamsides, and ditches across the U.S. and Canada. Looks like wild parsley or angelica. The root contains cicutoxin; even small ingestion causes seizures and death. Mistaken-identity poisonings happen when foragers confuse it for edible relatives. **Do not eat anything from the parsley family in the wild.** Symptoms (nausea, abdominal pain, seizures) start within 15–60 minutes; death within hours is possible. Activate emergency response immediately.

## Poison hemlock (Conium maculatum) — historic poison of Socrates
Tall (3–10 feet), white umbrella flowers, fern-like leaves, **smooth stem with purple blotches** (distinguishing it from Queen Anne's lace, which has hairy stems). All parts are toxic. Symptoms: weakness, paralysis, respiratory failure. Common roadside weed across the U.S.

## Manchineel (Hippomane mancinella) — only in southern Florida and the Caribbean
Small tropical tree, sometimes called "the little apple of death." Every part is toxic. The sap blisters skin and blinds eyes; the apple-like fruit is poisonous; even standing under it in the rain (sap dripping with water) burns skin. Marked with red rings at some Florida state parks. Do not approach.

## Devil's club (Oplopanax horridus) — Pacific Northwest
Large, broad maple-like leaves with palmately spreading lobes, on stems covered in sharp, brittle, **yellow spines** that snap off in skin and can cause infections. Common in moist forests of Alaska, BC, WA, OR, and northern ID. Treat puncture wounds: clean thoroughly, remove visible spine fragments with tweezers, monitor for infection.

## Pacific yew, oleander, foxglove, white snakeroot — known cardiac toxins
All cause severe cardiac symptoms if eaten. Foxglove (digitalis) is a common garden escapee. Oleander grows in the warm South and West. Never eat any unidentified flower or berry.

## What to do if exposure or ingestion is suspected
1. **Get away from the plant**; remove any contaminated clothing.
2. **Skin contact**: wash with soap and cold water; cover from sunlight (especially with hogweed/parsnip).
3. **Ingestion**: call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the U.S.) immediately if you have signal. Otherwise, activate PLB / satellite communicator. Do not induce vomiting unless told to by Poison Control. If the person is unconscious, lay them on their side.
4. **Photograph the plant** if you can do so safely — identification accelerates treatment.
