# Manchineel — the World's Most Dangerous Tree

## Where it lives
Manchineel (Hippomane mancinella) grows along Caribbean and Atlantic coasts including the Florida Keys, southern coastal Florida, parts of the Bahamas, much of the Caribbean, and northern South America. It's a medium-sized evergreen tree, often seen on beaches and in coastal hammocks. The name "manzanilla de la muerte" means "little apple of death" in Spanish — earned.

## Identification
A tree 20–50 feet tall with small, oval, glossy green leaves and small greenish-yellow flowers. Fruit looks like small green apples — 1–2 inches across, sometimes ripening yellow. Bark is grayish, becoming reddish in cracks. Often found near beach dunes and in mangrove edges. In Florida and the US Caribbean territories, manchineel trees are sometimes marked with red bands or "Toxic — do not touch" signs by parks departments. If you see those warnings, take them seriously.

## What makes it dangerous
Every part of the tree contains toxic sap. Leaves dripping rainwater are toxic to skin and eyes. The sap on hands transferring to face causes severe pain and blistering. The fruit causes severe ulceration of mouth and throat if eaten. Smoke from burning manchineel wood is a respiratory hazard and can blind people downwind. Death from eating the fruit is documented but rare; serious injury is common.

## Do not touch, do not stand under
Don't lean against, touch, or rest under a manchineel during rain — water dripping from leaves carries enough sap to blister skin. Don't camp under one. Don't burn its wood. Don't pick up dropped fruit or leaves. Hands that contacted sap should be washed with soap and copious water immediately; transferring sap to eyes is far worse than skin contact. Some local people have learned to harvest the wood safely for furniture; this is not a skill for visitors.

## If you contact sap
Wash skin thoroughly with soap and water, multiple times. Cool compresses for pain and blistering. Severe contact, contact with eyes, or any ingestion is a medical emergency — evacuate to care. Contact dermatitis can develop hours after exposure. Anyone who burned manchineel or stood downwind of burning manchineel should seek medical care for respiratory symptoms even if mild.

## Other tropical hazards
The Caribbean and tropical coasts have several other toxic plants outdoor visitors should know: pencil tree (Euphorbia tirucalli) with toxic latex, lethal jellyfish (sea wasp, box jellyfish) in some waters, manchioneel relatives in central American forests, cone snails on Pacific reefs. Trip to unfamiliar tropical areas: read local park materials, ask rangers, defer to local knowledge. If a plant or animal looks tropical and you don't recognize it, don't touch it.
