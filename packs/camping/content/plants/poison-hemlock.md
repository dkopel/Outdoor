# Poison Hemlock and Water Hemlock

## Why these matter
Poison hemlock (Conium maculatum) and water hemlock (Cicuta species) are among the deadliest plants in North America. Water hemlock is sometimes called "the most violently toxic plant in North America" — a small bite of root can kill an adult. Both look unsettlingly similar to wild carrot, wild parsnip, parsley, and other edible plants in the carrot family (Apiaceae). This is exactly why this app refuses to identify wild plants as safe to eat.

## Poison hemlock identification
A tall plant (3–10 feet at maturity), with smooth hollow stems marked by distinctive purple-red spots or streaks (no other carrot-family plant has these). Leaves are finely divided, fern-like. White flowers in umbrella-shaped clusters in late spring and early summer. Crushed leaves smell unpleasant, often described as "mousy." Found along roadsides, fence lines, stream banks, and disturbed ground throughout North America. All parts are toxic; toxicity does not decrease with drying or cooking.

## Water hemlock identification
Smaller than poison hemlock (2–6 feet). Stems are hollow with chambered roots (cut a root open lengthwise — chambers are diagnostic). Leaves are pinnately divided. White flowers in umbrella clusters. Found in wet places: marshes, stream banks, wet meadows. Roots smell like raw parsnip or carrot — easy to mistake for an edible. Highest toxicity in early spring when roots are most active.

## Symptoms of poisoning
Hemlock poisoning starts within 15 minutes to 2 hours: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, dilated pupils, weakness, slow heart rate, paralysis starting in the legs and ascending. Water hemlock can also cause violent seizures. Death is from respiratory failure when paralysis reaches breathing muscles. There is no antidote — supportive medical care (breathing support, seizure control) is the only treatment. Anyone suspected of hemlock exposure needs immediate emergency medical care.

## Why you can't trust ID by appearance alone
Both species look almost exactly like wild carrot, parsley, parsnip, fennel, anise, and several other common edible plants from a glance. Even experienced foragers misidentify carrot-family plants and die. The chambered roots of water hemlock are diagnostic but you have to dig and cut — and that's already too risky. Do not eat any wild plant from the carrot family without ironclad ID from multiple sources, ideally including chemical or DNA confirmation. For most outdoor people: just don't eat from this family. The risk reward is bad.

## What to do if exposure suspected
If a person ate any portion of a suspected hemlock or its look-alikes, evacuate immediately to medical care. Do not induce vomiting unless instructed by Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the US). Bring the plant — root, stem, leaves, flowers — for ID. Time is critical; toxins can act in under an hour. Even small ingestions warrant medical evaluation.
