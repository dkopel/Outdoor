# Oleander

## Where it grows
Oleander is a popular ornamental shrub across the southern US — California, Texas, Florida, Arizona, the Gulf Coast — used in highway landscaping and gardens for its hardiness and showy flowers. It's evergreen, drought-tolerant, and grows wild as an escape in some areas. Every part of the plant is toxic: leaves, flowers, stems, sap, and even the smoke when burned. Despite being decorative, it's one of the most toxic ornamental plants in cultivation.

## Identification
A large shrub or small tree, 6–20 feet tall. Long narrow leathery leaves arranged in whorls of three around the stem (sometimes opposite pairs). Clusters of pink, white, red, or yellow showy flowers, often with a fragrance. Long thin seed pods that split open to release fluffy-tufted seeds. The plant exudes a thick sticky white sap when broken. Often pruned into hedges or single specimens in landscaping.

## Why it kills
Oleander contains cardiac glycosides (oleandrin and others) chemically similar to digitalis. The mechanism of poisoning is the same — heart rhythm disturbances, cardiac arrest. Reports of fatalities from: eating one leaf as a tea, sucking on a flower stem out of curiosity, using oleander wood as a hot dog roasting stick (the sap and smoke contaminate the food), inhaling smoke from burning oleander branches.

## Don't burn oleander
A particularly dangerous mode of poisoning: people clearing brush who burn oleander and inhale smoke. The toxins are not destroyed by burning. Smoke inhalation can cause cardiac symptoms identical to ingestion. Never burn oleander branches in a fireplace, fire pit, or open burn pile. Bag and remove cuttings; some municipalities have specific oleander disposal requirements.

## Symptoms
Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain within 1–4 hours. Slow or irregular heart rate, low blood pressure, palpitations, visual disturbances (similar to digitalis). Severe cases progress to cardiac arrest. Children are at highest risk because even small ingestions are dangerous. Pets — especially dogs and horses — are also frequently poisoned.

## What to do
Immediate evacuation to emergency care. Call Poison Control. Bring a plant sample. Treatment is similar to digitalis poisoning: cardiac monitoring, activated charcoal early, sometimes the same Digibind antidote used for digitalis. Outcomes are good if treatment is started early; delays are dangerous. Do not induce vomiting unless specifically instructed.

## In the field
You're unlikely to encounter oleander in true backcountry. The risk is at trailheads, parking areas, and camp parks in the South where it's used ornamentally. Don't let curious children pick flowers or chew leaves. Don't carve sticks from oleander branches for roasting. If a fire ring has unfamiliar wood and you're in the South, ask before burning. Educate the group at the start of southern trips.
