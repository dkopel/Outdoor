# Mountain Laurel, Rhododendron, and Azalea

## Why they matter
These showy evergreen shrubs dominate large parts of the Appalachian, Pacific Northwest, and Southern US understory. They contain grayanotoxins, which affect the heart and nervous system. Humans rarely die from contact, but children, livestock, and dogs are at higher risk. Honey made from bees that collected nectar primarily from these plants ("mad honey") can poison consumers — historically a recurring problem in Turkey and Nepal where Rhododendron ponticum dominates.

## Identification
**Mountain laurel (Kalmia latifolia)**: evergreen shrub or small tree, 5–15 feet, glossy leathery leaves, distinctive star-shaped pink-white flowers in late spring with anthers held in dimples on the petals (releasing when touched). State flower of Connecticut and Pennsylvania.

**Rhododendron**: many species, mostly evergreen, large leathery leaves, dense flower clusters in spring (pink, white, purple, red). Often huge — some Pacific Northwest specimens reach 20+ feet.

**Azalea**: closely related, smaller leaves, often deciduous, flowers in showy clusters in spring. Native and cultivated species across most of the US.

## Symptoms of poisoning
Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, weakness, dizziness, low blood pressure, slow heart rate, tingling around the mouth, sweating. Severe cases (especially in children or animals): seizures, coma, cardiac arrhythmias. Symptoms appear within hours of ingestion and typically resolve within 24 hours with supportive care.

## Real-world risk in the backcountry
You're rarely poisoned by touching these plants — the risk is eating leaves, flowers, or honey made from them. Children playing in "laurel hells" (dense thickets of mountain laurel) sometimes try the flowers. Wood from these plants, used to roast food, can taint the food with toxins. Honey from beehives near monoculture rhododendron stands can be toxic; commercial honey from these areas is typically diluted with honey from other sources, making severe poisonings rare in retail products.

## What to do
Treat ingestions as urgent medical events: call Poison Control, evacuate to medical care. Most healthy adults recover with supportive care (fluids, antiemetics, cardiac monitoring). Children, elderly, and people with heart disease may need more intensive treatment. There is no specific antidote. Bring a plant sample for identification.

## Pet danger
Dogs that browse on these plants can be severely poisoned. A mouthful of leaves can cause sustained vomiting, weakness, and heart rhythm changes. Don't let dogs forage in dense rhododendron or laurel. If a dog has eaten any amount, contact a veterinarian or the ASPCA poison control line — emergency care can be life-saving.

## "Laurel hell" considerations
In the Appalachians, "laurel hells" are thickets so dense you literally cannot walk through them. Camping inside is undesirable for many reasons (no flat ground, no breeze, often wet, snake habitat). The plants themselves aren't dangerous to walk past — just be aware that wood gathered for fires shouldn't include them, and kids should be told not to chew leaves.
