# Snowmelt and Cold-Weather Water

## Melting snow takes more fuel than people expect
Snow is mostly air — a quart-size pot packed with snow yields about a cup of water. Plan extra fuel: roughly double what you'd carry for liquid-water trips of the same length. Start melting early; don't expect water on demand. A handful of snow eaten cold uses body heat to melt, cools your core, and barely hydrates — always melt it first.

## Melting technique
Start with a small amount of liquid water in the bottom of the pot if at all possible; snow alone scorches the pot bottom. Pack snow loosely; tightly packed snow melts slowly. Stir as it melts. Once water is established, add more snow. Pull the pot off the stove when water is hot but not boiling — that's enough to make hot drinks and rehydrate food, and it conserves fuel. For drinking water, you can melt without bringing to a boil if you're confident the snow is clean (well above any pollution, no animal tracks visible).

## Snow quality
Clean snow falls from clean sky. Surface snow on popular trails can contain animal waste, urine yellow, pine needles, dust. Dig down through the top inch or two to fresher snow underneath. Snow near established camps is sometimes used as a bathroom; ask before scooping. In areas with mining tailings or industrial runoff, snow can carry heavy metals — boil and filter as much as possible, and seek out cleaner sources where possible. Alpine snow far from human or animal use is generally fine.

## Storage in cold
Liquid water freezes. Strategies: keep bottles inside your pack against your back (warmer than side pockets); store bottles upside down so the freezing top isn't where you drink; sleep with your water in your sleeping bag overnight; insulate bottles with neoprene covers; carry a wide-mouth bottle (narrow mouths freeze first). Hydration bladders freeze through their hose in minutes; drain bladders and store dry in deep cold, or use insulated hose covers and blow water back into the bladder after each sip.

## Cold-weather hydration discipline
You feel less thirsty in cold. Your kidneys produce more urine (cold-induced diuresis). Dehydration in cold weather is widespread among hikers, and it worsens hypothermia risk. Drink on a schedule: 4–6 oz every 30 minutes when moving. Hot drinks at breaks are calorically expensive but psychologically valuable; budget fuel and time for them. Pee color: aim for pale yellow as in warm weather.

## Don't eat plain snow
A liter of snow eaten cold uses about 80 kcal of body heat to melt — significant in cold weather. It also chills the GI tract and triggers more shivering. The only reason to eat plain snow is true emergency dry-mouth relief, and even then take small amounts. Melt it first, even if you have to do it slowly in body-warmed water bottles inside layers.
