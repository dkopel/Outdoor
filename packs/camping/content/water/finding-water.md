# Finding Water

## Read the terrain
Water moves downhill. Follow drainages, valleys, and the green seams that snake through dry landscapes — vegetation often marks subsurface water. Game trails frequently lead to water. Aspect matters: in arid country, north-facing slopes (south-facing in the Southern Hemisphere) hold moisture longer and grow heavier vegetation. Bedrock depressions, large boulder bases, and the inside curves of dry streambeds collect water after rain — dig down a foot or two and let groundwater seep in.

## Springs and seeps
Springs are the gold standard — water emerging from the ground after passing through soil and rock has been crudely filtered. Look for them at the base of cliffs, where rock layers meet, or in the head of a drainage. Even so, treat the water; mammals upstream can contaminate any source. A "seep" — water that beads up rather than flows — is often drinkable after filtering; dig a small pit and let water collect, then scoop carefully without stirring up sediment.

## Dew and rainwater
A clean cloth dragged across grass at dawn picks up surprising quantities of dew; wring into a container. Tarps angled into a low point catch rain effectively — a tarp the size of a kitchen towel is enough to gather usable water in a heavy shower. Tree drip from broad leaves during light rain pools at low points on the trunk. Treat dew and rain like you'd treat any surface water if you have doubts; in most environments, freshly fallen rain is safer than ground water.

## Snow and ice
Snow is mostly air, so a pot of snow yields a small cup of water — plan ahead and start melting early. Pack snow lightly into a pot with a little water already in the bottom to keep it from scorching, and stir as it melts. Eating snow cools you and uses calories — melt it instead. Sea ice older than a year and freshwater ice are both fine sources; new sea ice retains salt. After melting, treat just like other surface water unless you sourced it from clean, fresh ice high in a glacial drainage.
