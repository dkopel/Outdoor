# Storing Water in the Field

## Bottle vs bladder tradeoffs
Bottles (1-quart Nalgene, 1-liter Smartwater, etc.) are simple, easy to clean, easy to fill from shallow sources, and survive cold and abuse. Bladders (3-liter Platypus, Camelbak) carry more for the same pack volume, ride flat in the back panel, and let you sip on the move. Bladders are harder to clean, can puncture, and freeze easily through their hose. Common practice: one bladder for trail sipping plus one bottle for camp cooking and as a backup. Many ultralight hikers carry only Smartwater bottles for both purposes.

## Cleaning containers
Bottles get a film of bacteria + saliva + drink-mix sugars. Wash with hot soapy water at the end of each trip, scrub the inside with a bottle brush, and let air-dry completely with the lid off. Sanitize occasionally with diluted unscented household bleach (1 teaspoon per gallon, let sit 5 minutes, rinse). Bladders need a thorough flush after every trip and full air-drying — humid bladders grow mold fast. Dedicated bladder brushes and hose brushes are worth their small weight.

## Cold weather
Liquid water freezes. Strategies that work: keep bottles inside the pack against your back, not in side pockets where wind cools them; sleep with your water bottle in your sleeping bag overnight; carry insulated bottle covers; use wide-mouth bottles (small openings freeze first); store bottles upside-down so the freezing top isn't where you drink from. Drink hot fluids from a thermos in deep cold. Bladders should be drained and stored dry at very low temperatures — ice cracks bladder seams.

## Pre-trip water for vehicles and basecamps
For car camps and basecamps, larger jerry-style jugs (5–7 gallons) work for cooking and dishes. Keep them in shade and turn them so the unused water mixes occasionally. Stored backup water in vehicles should be rotated every 6 months — stored municipal water in clean sealed containers stays drinkable for that long; longer in cool conditions; less in heat. Don't fill stored containers from a hose — fill from a tap.

## Sourcing into containers
Fill from upstream of where animals access water and downstream of any obvious contamination. Avoid silty water if you can; let cloudy water settle in a container for an hour and pour off the clear top. Pre-filter through a clean bandana to keep grit out of your real filter. Tip the bottle face-down to fill below the surface — surface scum and floating debris go onto the bottle rather than into the water.
