# Daily Water Budget

## Baseline needs
A sedentary adult needs about 2–3 liters of fluid per day, much of which comes from food. Active backpacking in moderate conditions: 3–4 liters of drinking water plus what's in food. Hot days with heavy effort: 5–6 liters, sometimes more. Cold weather: water needs don't drop as much as people think — dry air at altitude pulls moisture out of you with every breath. Add about 1 liter per 2,000 feet of elevation gain in dry climates.

## Plan water carry
Carry between confirmed water sources. Know where each is on your map and have a backup plan if a source is dry — in late summer, intermittent streams (blue dashed on USGS maps) often run dry. Water weighs about 2.2 pounds per liter; long carries between sources can add 10+ pounds to your pack. Choose routes that pass near water in dry regions, or plan a dry camp with enough capacity. Two-quart bottles + one bladder is a typical capacity for moderate trips; desert trips push toward 6+ liters.

## Recognize need before it's emergency
Thirst is a late signal. Better indicators of your hydration status: urine color (pale yellow = good, dark amber = bad, none for 6+ hours = serious), urine frequency (several times a day = ok), energy level (sudden flatness without good reason is often dehydration), headache, irritability. Cold and altitude both suppress thirst — drink on schedule, not on feeling. A practical baseline: half a liter per hour of moderate effort in moderate weather, more in heat.

## Cold-weather and altitude tactics
At elevation, the cold dry air, fast breathing, and diuretic effect of altitude all push water out. Insulate water bottles inside your pack (against your back, not in the outer pocket where they freeze). At very cold temps, store bottles upside down — water freezes top-down, so the lid stays openable longer. Use insulated hose covers on hydration bladders, or accept that bladders may freeze and switch to bottles for winter. Sip on a schedule rather than waiting for thirst.

## Electrolytes matter on long efforts
Drinking only plain water for 4+ hours of heavy sweat can dilute blood sodium dangerously (hyponatremia). Symptoms mimic dehydration but adding water makes it worse: headache, nausea, confusion. Add salty food, electrolyte tablets, or a sports drink mix to your routine on long hot days. A pinch of salt and a small sugar source per liter is the field minimum. Caffeinated drinks count toward fluid intake but aren't ideal as the bulk of it.
