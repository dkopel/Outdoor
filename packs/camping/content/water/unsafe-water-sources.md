# Recognizing Unsafe Water Sources

In the backcountry, treat **all** water from natural sources as potentially contaminated until purified — even fast-moving, clear-looking water at high elevation. Pathogens to worry about include *Giardia*, *Cryptosporidium*, *E. coli*, *Campylobacter*, viruses (norovirus, hepatitis A in some regions), and chemical or industrial contamination near roads and ranches.

## Sources that look safer than they are
- **High-elevation alpine streams**: usually clean of human pathogens but can carry giardia from upstream wildlife. Treat anyway.
- **Snowmelt**: pure-looking but may carry whatever was on or in the snowpack. Treat.
- **"Pristine"-looking lakes**: lakes concentrate everything that washed in. Treat.
- **Springs emerging directly from rock**: lowest-risk natural source, but still treat.

## Sources to avoid even after treatment
Some contamination cannot be removed by filtration or boiling. Avoid these unless absolutely no alternative exists, and even then consider the trade-offs:
- **Stagnant water with algae blooms** (green/red scum, foul smell). Cyanobacteria release toxins that boiling does not destroy.
- **Water downstream of mines, mills, or industrial sites**: may contain heavy metals (lead, mercury, arsenic). Filters and boiling do not remove these.
- **Water passing through agricultural fields or feedlots**: high pathogen load, plus pesticide and fertilizer runoff.
- **Water with a chemical smell**: hydrocarbons, sulfur.
- **Water with a strong rotten-egg smell** (hydrogen sulfide): unpleasant rather than dangerous in low doses, but indicates anaerobic source.
- **Hot springs**: variable thermophilic bacteria; some contain amoebae that cause fatal infection. Do not drink hot-spring water even when warm.
- **Salt or brackish water**: cannot be made safe by ordinary backcountry methods (only distillation works).

## Visual and smell warnings — but appearance is not enough
- **Cloudy, brown, or silty** water: pre-filter through a clean bandana or coffee filter to remove sediment before treating. Sediment reduces the effectiveness of chemical and UV treatment.
- **Foul smell**: rotten, sulfur, chemical, or septic odors mean find another source.
- **Visible animal carcasses upstream**: move at least 100 yards upstream and away from the contamination zone if you can; otherwise find another source.
- **Visible green/blue/red surface film**: do not drink, do not even swim.
- **Foam on the surface**: natural foam from churning water is fine; soap-like foam suggests contamination.

## Practical priorities
1. Best: moving water from springs or mid-stream, in remote drainages, away from grazing, mining, and downstream of campsites.
2. Acceptable: most alpine streams and lakes after proper treatment.
3. Last resort: cloudy or silty water — pre-filter, then treat with multiple methods.
4. Avoid: stagnant lakes with surface scum, water downstream of human or industrial impacts, water with chemical smells.
