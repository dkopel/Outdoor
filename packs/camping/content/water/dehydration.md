# Dehydration

## Recognizing it
Early signs: thirst, dark yellow or amber urine, dry mouth, headache, irritability, fatigue. Moderate: dizziness on standing, infrequent urination (more than 4 hours between bathroom stops in active heat), dry skin that doesn't bounce back when pinched, deepening color of urine. Severe: confusion, very rapid pulse, no urination for 8+ hours, sunken eyes, fainting. Heat and altitude both accelerate fluid loss — you can be dangerously dehydrated in cool weather at altitude without noticing.

## Daily intake guidelines
A reasonable starting point in moderate conditions is 2–3 liters per day for an average adult, plus 0.5–1 liter for every hour of moderate exertion in heat. Match output: pale yellow urine, several times a day, is the simplest indicator you're well hydrated. Cold and altitude both blunt thirst — drink on a schedule, not just when thirsty. Sweet drinks or sports drinks slow gastric emptying; plain or lightly electrolyte-flavored water absorbs faster.

## Electrolytes matter on long efforts
Sweating loses sodium along with water. Drinking only plain water during multi-hour efforts in heat can dilute blood sodium (hyponatremia) — symptoms mimic dehydration (headache, nausea, confusion) but adding more water makes it worse. Eat salty food and use electrolyte tablets or powder for any effort longer than ~2 hours or in significant heat. A small pinch of salt added to a liter of water (well under 1/4 teaspoon) is the field minimum.

## Rehydrating someone
For mild to moderate dehydration in a conscious person who can swallow, give small sips of cool electrolyte solution or water frequently — gulping triggers nausea. Add a pinch of salt and a small spoon of sugar to a liter of water if no commercial mix is available (oral rehydration solution principle). Severe dehydration with altered mental state, persistent vomiting, or inability to drink is a medical emergency — evacuate and call for help.
