# Penetrating Chest and Abdominal Wounds

## Why these are different
Penetrating injuries to the chest or abdomen can cause life-threatening internal damage with little outward bleeding. Standard "direct pressure" can be inappropriate (pushing on a chest wound can collapse a lung; pushing on exposed bowel can cause more harm than help). Recognizing and managing these injuries differently from limb wounds can save lives.

## Sucking chest wound
**Recognition**: a hole in the chest wall (front, side, or back of torso between collarbone and last rib) that bubbles, hisses, or "sucks" with breathing. May make a slurping or whistling sound on inhale or exhale. The patient often has trouble breathing, may show one side of the chest not expanding normally.

**The danger**: air enters the chest cavity through the wound, collapsing the lung (pneumothorax) and potentially developing into a tension pneumothorax (life-threatening pressure on the heart and other lung).

**Field treatment**:
1. Seal the wound with an occlusive dressing — a flat piece of plastic (clean food wrapper, glove, vapor barrier, commercial chest seal). Tape on three sides only — leave the fourth side untaped to vent.
2. The three-sided tape creates a one-way valve: air can escape on exhale, but the loose flap closes on inhale to prevent air entry.
3. If symptoms worsen (severe difficulty breathing, distended neck veins, blue lips) — "burp" the seal by lifting it briefly to release trapped air, then re-seal.
4. Position the patient — usually most comfortable sitting up or with injured side down.
5. Activate emergency evacuation immediately.

A commercial chest seal (Asherman or HyFin, included in some advanced first aid kits) is designed exactly for this — they have one-way valves built in. Worth carrying if your trip might involve gunshot or impalement risk (hunting trips, climbing where rockfall is real, etc.).

## Abdominal wounds
**Recognition**: a wound to the belly (any depth) — knife, branch, gunshot, severe blunt trauma. Signs of serious abdominal injury: visible exit of internal contents (bowel, mesentery, blood), severe pain that worsens, rigid tender abdomen, signs of shock, internal bleeding (rapid pulse, falling blood pressure, pale clammy skin).

**Field treatment**:
1. Do NOT push protruding organs back inside the body. They are contaminated and may be damaged.
2. Cover exposed organs with a clean, damp (sterile saline or clean water) cloth or dressing. Keep moist; drying causes serious tissue damage.
3. Cover with a clean dry outer layer to retain moisture and provide cover. Do not apply pressure.
4. Lay the patient flat on their back, knees bent up (this relaxes the abdominal wall).
5. Treat for shock (insulate, monitor).
6. Do NOT give food or water.
7. Activate emergency evacuation immediately.

## Penetrating object still in place
**Do not remove** any object impaled in the body. The object may be compressing a blood vessel or organ; pulling it out can release a fatal bleed. Stabilize the object so it doesn't move (cone of cloth or padding around it, taped in place). Pad on all sides. Evacuate with the object intact, telling rescuers and medical staff what's in there.

Exception: only remove if the object directly prevents you from doing CPR or maintaining the airway. Removal is a hospital procedure with surgical equipment available; in the field, stabilize.

## Treating for shock
Trauma patients almost always need treatment for shock alongside direct treatment of the wound:
- Lay flat on back unless breathing is hard (then sit up).
- Insulate from the ground.
- Keep warm.
- Monitor pulse, breathing, mental state every 5 minutes.
- Don't give food or water — anesthesia/surgery is likely needed.

## Triage and decisions
A severe chest or abdominal wound is at the top of the evacuation priority list. Use PLB / satellite SOS immediately. Helicopter evacuation can mean the difference between survival and death for these injuries — internal bleeding has no field treatment. Document time of injury, mechanism, vital signs over time.

## Outdoor mechanisms of penetrating trauma
- Falling on a stick or ski pole — common with downhill mountaineers.
- Sharp branch piercing during a fall through brush.
- Rockfall driving rocks into the body.
- Hunting accidents (gunshot, arrow).
- Knife slips during food prep or wood work.
- Bear or large-mammal goring.

For trips where any of these is plausible, carry a chest seal and have at least one group member with wilderness medicine training. The skill to recognize and treat these injuries is rarely needed, but when it is, it's everything.
