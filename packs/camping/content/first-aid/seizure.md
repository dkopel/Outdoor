# Seizures

## Recognize a generalized seizure
Sudden loss of consciousness, rigid muscles for 5–20 seconds, then rhythmic jerking of arms, legs, and face for 1–3 minutes. May be accompanied by crying out at onset, biting tongue (often), losing bladder or bowel control, irregular breathing, blue/gray lips during apnea, foam at the mouth. After the active phase, the person is typically confused, exhausted, and may sleep for 30 minutes to several hours ("postictal" state).

## Causes outdoors
Causes a person to seize in the backcountry: known epilepsy with missed medications (most common), severe head injury, hypoglycemia in a diabetic, severe electrolyte imbalance (dehydration, hyponatremia), heat stroke at extreme temperatures, severe hypothermia, water hemlock or other neurotoxin poisoning, alcohol withdrawal, lightning strike. A first-time seizure in an otherwise healthy person is uncommon but possible — almost always warrants medical evaluation.

## What to do DURING the seizure
1. **Protect the head and body**. Move sharp objects, rocks, fire, anything dangerous away from the person. Cushion the head with a folded jacket or hands.
2. **Time the seizure** — note the start time. Duration matters for medical decisions.
3. **Roll them onto their side** (recovery position) as soon as the muscle rigidity allows. This helps prevent choking on saliva or vomit.
4. **DO NOT put anything in their mouth**. Old advice about preventing tongue swallowing is wrong — you cannot swallow your tongue, and objects in the mouth break teeth and obstruct airways.
5. **DO NOT hold them down**. Trying to stop the jerking can dislocate shoulders and injure both you and them.
6. **DO NOT give food or water** until they are fully conscious and oriented.

## After the seizure
The person will be confused, possibly combative or scared. Stay with them. Talk calmly. Keep them on their side until they fully recover. Recovery typically takes 5–30 minutes for full orientation, sometimes longer. Headache, fatigue, sore muscles, and tongue bite are common.

## When to call for emergency
Call 911 / PLB / satellite SOS if:
- Seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes (status epilepticus — medical emergency).
- Person doesn't wake up after the seizure ends.
- Another seizure follows immediately.
- Seizure happened in water.
- Person is injured during the seizure.
- Person is pregnant.
- Person has diabetes or other medical conditions.
- First-time seizure in someone with no known epilepsy.
- Slow or labored breathing after the seizure.

## In the backcountry
Even a "routine" seizure in someone with known epilepsy is a backcountry emergency if you're far from care. The person may not be able to safely continue the trip; they may be at risk of a second seizure; they may have injured themselves. Plan evacuation: walk-out with assistance if minor, PLB activation if severe. Keep the person hydrated (when fully conscious), warm, and monitored.

## Lightning-strike seizures
Lightning strike victims sometimes have seizures from cardiac or neurological injury. Treat the same way (protect, time, side position), but lightning is also a CPR-priority indication if the heart has stopped. Multiple injuries are common in lightning strikes — look for burns, broken bones from falls, hearing damage.

## Pre-trip planning for known epilepsy
Anyone with known epilepsy should: tell the group leader pre-trip; carry their medications double-bagged and well-distributed (so a lost pack doesn't end the trip); avoid known seizure triggers (sleep deprivation, alcohol, flashing lights for photosensitive epilepsy); consider carrying emergency rescue medication (rectal diazepam for prolonged seizures, prescribed by their doctor). Group should know what to do.
