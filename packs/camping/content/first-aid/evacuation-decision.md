# Deciding Whether to Evacuate

## The three options
For any backcountry injury or illness, you have three choices: stay and self-treat, walk out (assisted evacuation), or call for rescue. The choice depends on the severity of the problem, the difficulty of the terrain, the distance to definitive care, weather, group strength, daylight, and what evacuation resources can reach you. There is rarely one right answer — but there is usually one obviously wrong answer, so start by ruling that out.

## Stay-in-place situations
Minor injuries that respond to rest (sprains improving with RICE, mild dehydration, blisters, small cuts), short-term illness like a 24-hour stomach bug in a stable person, and major injuries where moving the person would worsen them (suspected spinal injury, major femur fracture) and rescue can reach you. Build a comfortable shelter, treat the patient, mark the location for searchers (bright cloth, smoke fire, flashing light at night), and ration food and water. Send one person for help only if there are at least three left at camp.

## Walk-out (assisted evacuation)
Use when: the patient can walk with support, the route is reasonable, weather is OK, you have daylight or working lights, and the medical condition is unlikely to worsen sharply over the walk. Move slowly — half normal pace at most. Take frequent breaks. Carry their pack. Watch them for deterioration; a person who started walking can transition to a stay-and-call situation quickly. Have a turnaround point in mind ("if they can't make this saddle by 3 PM, we stop and call").

## Call-for-rescue triggers
Any of the following triggers an immediate call/PLB/satellite SOS if you can: unconsciousness or altered mental status, breathing difficulty unrelated to exertion, chest pain, suspected stroke or heart attack, suspected spinal injury, uncontrolled bleeding, severe head injury, broken femur or pelvis, anaphylaxis, suspected HACE or HAPE, severe burns, near-drowning, severe hypothermia, snakebite. Also: anyone showing rapidly worsening signs that you don't understand. The general rule: if you're asking "should we call?", you should already be calling.

## PLB / satellite messenger use
A Personal Locator Beacon or satellite communicator is the most reliable way to summon help in the backcountry. Activate from a clear-sky location away from cliffs or heavy canopy. Give the device 5–15 minutes to acquire satellites and transmit. Stay near the activation point — rescuers will go to that GPS coordinate. If you have two-way satellite text, send concise messages: location, number of people, nature of the emergency, condition of the patient, weather. Do not turn the device off until rescue arrives.

## After-action habits
Once rescue is initiated or the walk-out is decided, assign clear roles: who treats the patient, who navigates, who manages the group, who communicates with rescuers. Keep written notes of vital signs (pulse, breathing, skin signs) every 15 minutes — receiving medical staff will want a trend, and you won't remember six hours later. After every backcountry incident, debrief the group within a week. What worked, what didn't, what gear you wished you'd had.
