# Drowning

## Drowning is quiet
Real drowning rarely looks like the movies. Victims usually can't shout — their mouth alternates above and below water and their airway has to be used for breathing, not yelling. Arms instinctively press down on the water surface for leverage; they don't wave. Eyes are wide or closed. Head tipped back. The whole event often lasts 20–60 seconds. Watch children and tired swimmers continuously; people often drown within 10 feet of help.

## Reach, throw, row, go
Standard water-rescue priority order. **Reach**: extend a stick, paddle, branch, or your own arm to the victim while you stay anchored on shore. **Throw**: throw a buoyant object — life jacket, dry bag, cooler, even a coiled rope — and pull them in. **Row**: use any boat or floating platform to get to them and pull them aboard. **Go**: enter the water as a last resort, only with a flotation device for both of you. A drowning victim will grab you and push you under to keep their own head up — even strong swimmers die this way.

## Once you have the person out
Position them on their back. Tilt the head back, lift the chin, look-listen-feel for breathing for no more than 10 seconds. If not breathing or only gasping, start CPR immediately. The priority for drowning victims is rescue breaths — give 2 breaths first, then 30 compressions, then 2 breaths, and continue cycles. (Unlike sudden cardiac arrest where hands-only CPR is preferred, drowning victims often have an oxygen problem first.) Continue until they breathe normally, professional help arrives, or you cannot continue.

## After they revive
Anyone who lost consciousness, inhaled water, or required CPR must be evacuated to medical care, even if they seem completely fine afterward. "Secondary drowning" — fluid in the lungs causing breathing trouble — can develop hours later. Cold-water victims need rewarming and hypothermia management on top of post-rescue care. Watch for vomiting and protect the airway.

## Prevention is most of the answer
Wear a life jacket on moving water and on cold water, period. River currents are stronger than they look and shed body heat fast. Don't enter water you can't see the bottom of. Children always need supervision near any water deeper than a few inches. Alcohol and water do not mix. Practice swimming and water-rescue skills before you need them; reading is no substitute.
