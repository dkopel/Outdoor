# Nosebleeds in the Backcountry

## Why they happen outdoors
Dry air at altitude, cold temperatures, dehydration, blowing your nose hard, and trauma all increase nosebleeds. Most are minor and stop within 10–20 minutes with simple care. Persistent or recurrent nosebleeds in cold dry conditions are extremely common; pre-trip preparation (saline nasal spray, lip balm under the nostrils) helps.

## Stopping a typical nosebleed
1. Sit upright and lean slightly forward — do not tilt the head back. Tilting back sends blood down the throat, causing nausea, gagging, and inability to see when the bleeding stops.
2. Pinch the soft (lower, cartilaginous) part of the nose firmly between thumb and forefinger. Pinch the whole soft part, not just the tip.
3. Breathe through the mouth. Hold pressure for at least 10–15 minutes without letting go to check — releasing early restarts the clot.
4. Apply a cold compress to the bridge of the nose if available.
5. If bleeding hasn't stopped after 20 minutes of continuous pressure, repeat for another 10 minutes. If still bleeding after that, evacuate.

## After it stops
Avoid blowing your nose for several hours. Avoid lifting heavy items, straining, or sneezing forcefully for 12–24 hours. Lubricate the nostrils with a tiny amount of petroleum jelly or saline gel; dry blood crust falls off and restarts bleeds. Stay hydrated. Avoid hot drinks for a few hours after a nosebleed.

## When to evacuate
Evacuate for: bleeding that doesn't stop after 30 minutes of correct pressure; very heavy bleeding from both nostrils simultaneously; bleeding from the nose after a head injury (could indicate skull fracture); recurrent bleeds in someone on blood thinners; nosebleeds with weakness, dizziness, pale skin, or rapid pulse (signs of significant blood loss). Repeated severe altitude-induced nosebleeds may benefit from descending.

## Altitude-specific prevention
At elevation, the dry thin air dehydrates nasal membranes. Strategies that work: saline nasal spray several times a day (small bottle, weighs nothing), a thin smear of petroleum jelly inside the nostrils morning and night, drinking more water than feels necessary, using a buff or balaclava to humidify breathing in cold dry air. Climbing high altitudes after a known nosebleed history warrants extra prevention; some people have to come down.
