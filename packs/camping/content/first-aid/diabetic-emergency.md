# Diabetic Emergencies

## Two kinds, both dangerous
**Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar)** is far more common and develops fast — minutes. **Hyperglycemia (high blood sugar)** develops over hours to days and can progress to diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Both can be fatal if untreated. Field-treatable: hypoglycemia. Evacuation-required: severe hyperglycemia.

## Recognize hypoglycemia
Sudden onset over 15–30 minutes:
- Shakiness, sweating, pale clammy skin
- Confusion, irritability, anxiety
- Hunger
- Weakness, dizziness
- Rapid pulse, palpitations
- Slurred speech, uncoordinated movement (looks like intoxication)
- Severe: seizure, loss of consciousness

A known diabetic acting "off" in the backcountry — assume hypoglycemia until proven otherwise. Quick treatment is harmless if you're wrong; delayed treatment can be fatal if you're right.

## Treat hypoglycemia
If conscious and able to swallow safely:
- 15g fast-acting carbohydrate: 4 glucose tablets, half a regular soda (NOT diet), 4 ounces of juice, 1 tablespoon honey, 1 tube glucose gel.
- Wait 15 minutes, reassess.
- If still symptomatic, repeat the dose.
- Once symptoms resolve, give a complex carbohydrate + protein meal (bread + peanut butter, granola bar + nuts) to stabilize.

If unconscious or unable to swallow:
- Do NOT put food or liquid in their mouth — aspiration risk.
- If they have a glucagon injection kit (some diabetics carry one), administer per the kit's instructions (usually intramuscular thigh injection). They'll regain consciousness in 5–15 minutes.
- Place in recovery position.
- Activate emergency response.
- Once conscious, give carbs as above.

## Recognize hyperglycemia / DKA
Develops over hours to days:
- Excessive thirst, frequent urination
- Fatigue, weakness
- Nausea, vomiting
- Abdominal pain
- Deep rapid breathing ("Kussmaul respiration")
- Fruity / acetone breath odor
- Confusion progressing to coma
- Very high blood sugar on a meter (>250 mg/dL, often >400)

DKA is a medical emergency. Field treatment is limited; evacuate immediately. Give the patient water to sip if conscious — dehydration is a major component. Insulin without a meter and supervision is risky in the field.

## In the backcountry with diabetes
Pre-trip planning matters enormously:
- Pack glucose meter, test strips (extra), insulin (extra), syringes/pens.
- Pack rapid-acting carbs accessibly — multiple locations (chest pocket, hip belt, top of pack), so a single lost stash doesn't leave you stranded.
- Pack glucagon emergency kit if your doctor prescribes one.
- Test more often than at home — exertion, cold, heat, and stress all change insulin needs.
- Anticipate higher carb needs on hiking days.
- Tell the group: who carries what, what to do if you become unresponsive.

## Test strips and meters in cold
Test strips don't work below freezing and degrade quickly even at moderate cold. Keep them and the meter inside layers near body heat. Insulin freezes — store in an insulated container, never in the outer pocket on cold days. Frozen insulin is ruined and must be discarded.

## Insulin pumps and altitude
Insulin pumps can air-bubble at altitude as dissolved gas comes out of solution. Pre-trip, ask the manufacturer or your endocrinologist about how to handle altitude (Diabetes Camp protocols cover this; some pumps need priming/dis-priming with altitude changes). Tubing failures, occluded sets, and pump electronic issues all happen in remote travel — carry full backup insulin pen/syringe and basal/bolus protocol.

## CGMs (continuous glucose monitors)
CGMs (Dexcom, Libre) need sensor changes every 7–14 days. Pack spares. They can fail in cold or after impact. Don't trust them blindly in the backcountry; confirm with finger-stick if symptoms don't match the reading. Phone-tethered CGMs need the phone alive and the app working.
