# Heart Attack and Stroke

## Why this matters in the backcountry
Most outdoor adventurers are healthy, but heart attacks and strokes still occur — especially in older adventurers, those with known cardiovascular disease, those at altitude, and those pushing hard in heat or cold. Both are time-critical: every minute of delay reduces survival and increases permanent damage. Recognizing them and triggering immediate evacuation is one of the highest-impact field skills.

## Heart attack — recognize it
**Common signs**: chest pain or pressure (often described as "elephant on my chest," sometimes burning or squeezing), pain radiating to jaw, neck, shoulder, or arm (usually left). Shortness of breath, nausea, sweating, lightheadedness, sudden weakness. Pain typically lasts more than a few minutes or comes and goes.

**Women, diabetics, and elderly often present atypically**: fatigue, jaw pain, back pain, mild stomach discomfort, nausea — without classic chest pain. Don't dismiss vague "not feeling right" in older adventurers.

## Heart attack — what to do
1. Have the person sit or lie in a position of comfort, often slightly upright.
2. Stop all exertion. Even walking is too much.
3. Activate emergency response: PLB, satellite SOS, call 911, send a runner.
4. If they have prescribed nitroglycerin, help them take it per label.
5. If conscious, alert, not allergic, and able to swallow: aspirin (chew 1 adult or 4 baby aspirin). This is widely recommended; check for known aspirin allergy first.
6. Loosen tight clothing.
7. If they lose consciousness and stop breathing — start CPR. Use an AED if you have one (some park stations do).
8. Stay with them. Reassure. Be ready to do CPR if needed.

Do not give food or water. Do not let them walk out. Even if symptoms improve, they need ER evaluation.

## Stroke — recognize with FAST
**F — Face**: ask the person to smile. Is one side drooping?
**A — Arms**: ask them to raise both arms. Does one drift down or stay weak?
**S — Speech**: ask them to repeat a simple sentence. Is it slurred or confused?
**T — Time**: note the time symptoms started. Call 911 immediately.

Other stroke signs: sudden severe headache "worst of my life," sudden vision change (one or both eyes), sudden severe dizziness, sudden numbness on one side, sudden difficulty walking or balancing.

## Stroke — what to do
1. Note the time symptoms started. This is the single most important piece of information for the ER — clot-busting treatments are only effective within hours of onset.
2. Activate emergency response immediately.
3. Have the person lie down with head slightly elevated.
4. Loosen tight clothing.
5. Don't give food or water. Don't give aspirin unless directed (aspirin is for heart attack; for stroke it depends on what kind, which can't be determined in the field).
6. Don't let them get up or "walk it off."
7. Monitor breathing. CPR if they go into arrest.

## Cardiac arrest — different from heart attack
A heart attack is a circulation problem (blocked artery); the person is usually conscious. Cardiac arrest is an electrical problem (heart stops pumping effectively); the person collapses, unconscious, not breathing. Heart attacks can lead to cardiac arrest. The treatments differ:

**Heart attack** — keep them conscious, activate EMS, get them to definitive care fast.
**Cardiac arrest** — start CPR immediately, use AED if available, continue until rescue or signs of life.

## Backcountry realities
At altitude, in remote areas, definitive care may be hours or days away. If a remote person has classic heart attack or stroke signs, the situation is grave — but doing your part well still matters. Document everything (time symptoms started, what was given, vital signs over time). Modern wilderness rescue helicopters can sometimes reach patients in 2–4 hours; this is survivable for some heart attacks if EMS is activated immediately.

## Prevention thinking
Older adventurers with known heart disease: get a stress test pre-trip if your itinerary involves significant altitude or sustained exertion. Carry medications properly. Tell the group what to do. Don't push pace beyond what the body is signaling. Acclimatize properly at altitude. Stay hydrated. Drink in moderation if at all. The first hour of a heart attack is critical; staying ahead of the risk is far better than treating one in the field.
