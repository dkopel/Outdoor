# Shock

## What it is
Shock is a life-threatening drop in tissue perfusion — the body's organs aren't getting enough oxygenated blood. In the backcountry, the most common causes are major blood loss, severe burns, dehydration plus heat or cold injury, anaphylaxis, and serious trauma to the chest or abdomen. Untreated shock can kill in 30 minutes to a few hours. Anyone with significant injury or illness should be monitored for shock continuously.

## Recognize early shock
Earliest signs: anxiety, restlessness, the person says "something is wrong" but you can't see what. Skin becomes pale, cool, and clammy. Pulse becomes rapid (over 100 in an adult) and weak. Breathing speeds up. Capillary refill — press a fingernail until it whitens, release, normal pink returns in under 2 seconds — slows beyond 2 seconds. As shock deepens: confusion, low or unreadable blood pressure, weak or absent pulse at the wrist, deep slow breathing or gasps. Loss of consciousness is late and ominous.

## Field treatment
Treat the cause if you can — control bleeding, splint major fractures, treat anaphylaxis with epinephrine, give cool water for heat exhaustion. Lay the person flat, on their back, on an insulating pad off cold ground. Elevate legs about 12 inches if there's no spine, head, or leg injury. Cover them — shock victims lose heat fast even in mild weather, and hypothermia worsens shock badly. Monitor pulse and breathing every 5 minutes. Reassure them; people in shock respond to a calm voice.

## What not to do
Do not give food or drink — shock victims often need surgery, and a stomach full of food complicates anesthesia. Small sips of water are acceptable only for fully conscious people with mild shock and a long evacuation ahead. Do not give alcohol. Do not elevate the head — the brain needs blood flow. Do not let them stand up or walk if avoidable. Do not warm them so much that they sweat; aim for "comfortably warm," not hot.

## Evacuation
Shock is a fast evacuation priority. If you have signal, satellite messenger, or a PLB, use it now. Carry the person rather than walking them whenever possible. Note their pulse, breathing rate, and skin signs every 5–10 minutes during evacuation so receiving medical staff have a trend.
