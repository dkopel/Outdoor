# Frostbite

## Recognize the stages
Frostnip is the first stage: skin pales, feels numb or prickly, but the underlying tissue is still soft. Reversible by warming. Superficial frostbite (1st–2nd degree): skin is white, waxy, and firm on top but the tissue underneath still gives under gentle pressure. Blisters may form on rewarming. Deep frostbite (3rd–4th degree): skin and tissue are hard all the way through, color is white-gray to purplish-black, no sensation. Tissue can be lost. Fingers, toes, nose, cheeks, and ears are the most common sites.

## Field treatment — superficial
Get the person out of cold and wind into shelter. Remove wet or constricting clothing and jewelry from the affected area before swelling sets in. Rewarm in warm (not hot) water at 99–102°F (37–39°C) — about the temperature of comfortable bath water on an uninjured forearm. The water should feel warm, never hot. Rewarming takes 15–30 minutes and is intensely painful as feeling returns; pain is a good sign that tissue is alive. Do not rub the frozen area; ice crystals tear tissue.

## Field treatment — deep frostbite
Deep frostbite is a medical emergency. Do not attempt to rewarm in the field if there's any chance the tissue will refreeze before reaching definitive care — refreezing after thaw is far more destructive than staying frozen. If you can keep the person and the affected tissue warm continuously through evacuation, gentle warm-water rewarming is appropriate. If you can't, splint, pad, insulate, and evacuate frozen. Do not walk on frostbitten feet if alternatives exist.

## Never do these
Never rub frostbitten tissue with snow or anything else. Never use direct heat (fire, stove, hair dryer) — the numb tissue can be burned without the person feeling it. Never break blisters. Never give alcohol or tobacco. Do not allow the person to use the affected part for normal tasks until evaluated medically.

## Prevention
Stay dry. Eat and drink — calories produce internal heat. Cover face, hands, and feet in wind chill below -10°F (-23°C). Take off and rewarm any extremity that goes numb; don't push through and assume it'll come back on its own. Check companions' faces for white patches every 15 minutes in serious cold. Avoid tight constricting boots, gaiters, or wrist watches that cut off circulation.
