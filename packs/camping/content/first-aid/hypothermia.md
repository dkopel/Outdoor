# Hypothermia

## Recognize the signs
Mild hypothermia (~95–93°F core): shivering, slurred speech, clumsy hands, the "umbles" (mumbles, fumbles, stumbles). The person can usually still respond and follow simple instructions. Moderate hypothermia (~92–86°F): shivering may stop, confusion increases, movements become uncoordinated. Severe hypothermia (below ~86°F): unconscious, very slow or undetectable breathing and pulse. Any altered mental state in a cold environment is hypothermia until proven otherwise.

## Stop ongoing heat loss first
Get the person out of wind and rain or off snow. Replace wet clothing with dry layers, including a dry hat. If you cannot fully change clothes, at minimum put dry insulation between them and the ground. Wrap them in any available insulation: sleeping bag, jackets, emergency blanket. The single biggest mistake is "warming them up" before stopping further cooling.

## Rewarm gently
For mild hypothermia, sugary or warm (not hot) liquids and high-calorie food help — but only if the person can swallow safely. Skin-to-skin contact inside a sleeping bag is effective and safe. Chemical heat packs or warm water bottles wrapped in cloth can be placed at the neck, armpits, and groin. Do not rub the limbs; do not immerse in hot water; do not give alcohol.

## Severe hypothermia is a medical emergency
A severely hypothermic person may appear dead — very slow pulse, barely-detectable breathing, rigid muscles — but can sometimes be revived with full hospital rewarming. Handle them gently (rough movement can trigger cardiac arrest). Begin gentle external rewarming, protect them from further cold, and evacuate as fast as the terrain allows. CPR is appropriate if no pulse is detected after a careful 30–60 second check.
