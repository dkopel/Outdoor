# Trench Foot (Immersion Foot)

## What it is
Trench foot — formally "immersion foot" — is tissue damage from prolonged exposure to wet, cool conditions, even above freezing. Hours to days of wet feet at temperatures from about 30°F to 60°F can damage skin, nerves, and small blood vessels. Common in soldiers (WWI trench warfare, hence the name), thru-hikers in wet weather, hunters wading streams, anyone with persistent wet feet in fall conditions.

## Symptoms
Early stages: numbness and tingling in feet, skin pale or blotchy, "pins and needles" sensation. Feet feel cold or numb even when warmed. Progressive stages: feet become red, swollen, painful when warmed (intense throbbing). Skin becomes blistered, then peels and ulcerates. In severe cases, deep tissue death — similar to frostbite but at warmer temperatures.

Crucially, the damage often becomes apparent only AFTER the feet are warmed and dried. You may walk all day with feet numb and "fine," then realize at camp that the skin is mottled and intensely painful. This is the dangerous moment.

## Field treatment
1. **Get the feet dry**. This is the single most important step. Even an hour of dryness slows progression.
2. **Rewarm gently** — don't soak in hot water (vasodilation can worsen tissue damage). Cover with warm dry socks, warm dry sleeping bag.
3. **Elevate** the feet slightly to reduce swelling.
4. **Do not rub** — friction damages compromised tissue.
5. **Treat any blisters** by leaving intact ones alone, draining popped ones cleanly (see first-aid.wound-care).
6. **Anti-inflammatory pain reliever** per label, if they tolerate it.
7. **Monitor for infection**: spreading redness, increasing warmth, pus, fever.

## When to evacuate
Severe trench foot, large areas of blistered or peeling skin, dark/blue tissue (deep injury), severe pain unrelieved by basic care, signs of infection. The patient may be unable to walk comfortably; evacuate by carrying or assisting. Tissue damage is sometimes permanent — early definitive care matters.

## Prevention is most of the answer
- **Dry feet are everything**. Change socks midday if wet from stream crossings, sweat, or rain — even just 30 minutes of dry-out helps.
- **Carry 2–3 pairs of hiking socks** on multi-day trips.
- **Wool or wool-blend socks** retain insulation when damp; synthetic dries faster; cotton kills feet in wet conditions.
- **Gaiters** prevent water from running into boots from above.
- **Vapor-barrier socks** in extremely wet/cold conditions (thin plastic between liner and outer sock) keep boot insulation dry by accepting that the inner liner gets wet — controversial technique, used in extreme cold.
- **At camp**, change to dry socks and let your hiking socks dry overnight in a warm place inside the bag.
- **Air feet out** at long breaks — take boots off, dry skin, fresh socks.

## Foot care discipline
Look at your feet at the end of every wet day. Pale, mottled, numb feet warrant immediate attention even if you "feel fine." Wash feet at camp if water allows, dry thoroughly, sleep in dry socks. Trench foot is far easier to prevent than to treat — the discipline of foot care in wet conditions pays off massively.

## In rain pants and gaiters
Constant moisture from inside (sweat) is also a trench foot risk. On long wet days, boots fill with sweat even from "dry" feet, especially in cooler weather where evaporation is low. Stop, remove boots, wring out socks, air feet for 10 minutes — this single break can prevent damage on a 12-hour wet day. Trail-runner-style trips in summer rain often go fine because the lighter shoe drains and the conditions warm; multi-day in cool wet conditions in heavy boots is when trench foot strikes hardest.
