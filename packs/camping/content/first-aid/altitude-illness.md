# Altitude Illness

## Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)
AMS typically appears 6–12 hours after arriving above ~8,000 ft (2,400 m). Symptoms: headache, nausea or loss of appetite, fatigue beyond what the day's effort explains, dizziness, and disturbed sleep. AMS feels like a hangover at altitude. Most cases are mild and resolve with rest and good hydration over 12–24 hours. The cardinal rule: do not go higher with worsening symptoms — descend if symptoms intensify, never push toward the summit through clear AMS.

## High Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE)
HACE is a life-threatening progression of AMS — swelling of the brain. Signs: ataxia (unable to walk a straight line, heel-to-toe test fails), confusion, irrational behavior, severe headache that doesn't respond to rest. HACE can kill within hours. Immediate descent of at least 1,000–3,000 ft is the only effective field treatment. Do not wait for daylight, do not wait for "it might improve." Carry the person if necessary.

## High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)
HAPE is fluid in the lungs at altitude. Signs: severe shortness of breath at rest (not just exertion), wet or gurgling cough, chest tightness, weakness, blue-tinged lips or fingernails, very rapid pulse. The person sounds wet and labored even sitting still. Like HACE, immediate descent is the field treatment — descending 1,000–3,000 ft often produces dramatic improvement. HAPE can also kill within hours. Bottled oxygen and certain prescription medications help but do not substitute for descent.

## Acclimatization rules
The standard mountaineering rule: above ~10,000 ft, do not increase your sleeping altitude by more than ~1,500 ft (500 m) per day. Take a rest day every 3,000 ft of net sleeping-altitude gain. "Climb high, sleep low" — day-hiking higher than your camp is fine and helps. Hydrate well. Avoid alcohol and sedatives at altitude. Eat carbohydrates — the body burns them more efficiently in thin air.

## Prevention and red flags
Plan itineraries with built-in acclimatization days. Never go from sea level to sleeping above 9,000 ft in a single day if you have any choice. Genetics is real — fitness doesn't predict altitude tolerance, and someone who got AMS last trip will likely get it again. Red flags requiring immediate descent: any ataxia, any rest-shortness-of-breath, vomiting that won't stop, severe headache unresponsive to rest. Going down even 1,000 ft can save a life.
