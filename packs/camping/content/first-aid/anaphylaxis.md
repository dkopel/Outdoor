# Anaphylaxis

## Recognize it fast
Anaphylaxis is a whole-body allergic reaction that can kill in minutes. Common triggers in the outdoors: bee/wasp stings, food (peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish), medications, latex. Signs come on within seconds to 30 minutes of exposure: widespread hives or rash, swelling of the lips/tongue/face, throat tightness or hoarse voice, wheezing or noisy breathing, vomiting or diarrhea, sudden weakness or dizziness, low blood pressure or loss of consciousness. Any two or more body systems involved = treat as anaphylaxis. Trust skin + breathing or skin + circulation combinations especially.

## Use epinephrine immediately if available
If the person has a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen, Auvi-Q, generic), help them use it without delay. Inject into the outer thigh muscle, through clothing if necessary. Hold in place for the time the device specifies (usually 3–10 seconds). Note the time. A second dose may be needed in 5–15 minutes if symptoms persist or return — many auto-injector prescriptions come as a two-pack for exactly this reason. Epinephrine is the only treatment that reverses anaphylaxis in the field. Antihistamines like diphenhydramine are not a substitute.

## Position and protect
After epinephrine: lay the person flat with legs elevated about 12 inches, unless they are vomiting (then on their side) or having difficulty breathing (then sitting up is fine, do not force flat). Loosen tight clothing. Do not give them anything to eat or drink unless they're fully alert and not at risk of throwing up. If unconscious and not breathing, start CPR.

## Evacuate, even if they get better
Anaphylactic reactions can return ("biphasic reaction") hours later, even after dramatic improvement. Every anaphylaxis episode needs medical evaluation. Get the person to definitive care as fast as conditions allow. Carry the used auto-injector with you and tell receiving medical staff exactly when it was given, and whether a second dose was needed.

## If you carry epinephrine for yourself
Keep the auto-injector accessible — chest pocket, hip belt, top of pack, not buried at the bottom. Tell your group what it is and where it is on day one. Replace at expiration (the medicine degrades). Practice removing the safety cap on a trainer device. If you have a known severe allergy and refuse to carry epinephrine into the backcountry, you are gambling with your life — there is no field workaround.
