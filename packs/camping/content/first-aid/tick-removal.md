# Tick Removal

Ticks transmit Lyme disease, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, and several other illnesses. The risk of disease transmission rises sharply after a tick has been attached for 24–36 hours. Prompt, correct removal dramatically reduces your risk.

## Equipment
The right tool is **fine-tipped tweezers** (sharper than household tweezers) or a purpose-built tick-removal tool (small notched plastic key style). If neither is available, regular tweezers work. Avoid using your fingers if possible — fingers squeeze the tick's body and can inject more pathogens.

## How to remove a tick (CDC method)
1. **Grasp the tick** as close to the skin as possible. Get the mouthparts, not the body.
2. **Pull straight up** with steady, even pressure. Do not twist. Do not jerk. Do not squeeze the body — squeezing pumps the tick's gut contents (and any pathogens) into you.
3. The tick may take 10–30 seconds of steady pull to release. If mouthparts break off and stay in the skin, leave them alone — they will work out on their own, like a splinter. Do not dig.
4. **Dispose of the tick**: seal it in tape, drop it in a sealed bag or container, drop it in rubbing alcohol, or flush it down a toilet. Do not crush it with your fingers.
5. **Clean the bite area** and your hands with soap and water, rubbing alcohol, or hand sanitizer.

## What NOT to do
- Do not use petroleum jelly, nail polish, or other substances to "smother" the tick.
- Do not apply heat (a match, a hot needle) to the tick.
- Do not twist, jerk, or wiggle the tick out.
- Do not crush the tick body during removal.

All of these increase the chance the tick regurgitates pathogens into the bite.

## After removal
- Note the date of the bite and where on your body it was attached.
- If possible, save the tick (sealed in tape or a small container) for at least 30 days. If you become ill, the species and possibly its testing can help diagnosis.
- Take a photo of the bite location.
- Watch for symptoms over the next 30 days.

## When to see a doctor
Seek medical evaluation if you develop any of these in the 30 days after a tick bite:
- A rash, especially the classic "bullseye" of Lyme disease (a red ring expanding outward from the bite over days)
- Fever, chills, body aches, fatigue
- Headache or stiff neck
- Joint pain or swelling
- The tick was attached for over 24 hours and you live in or visited a high-risk Lyme area

In high-risk areas, a single dose of doxycycline within 72 hours of removal can prevent Lyme disease in some cases — your physician decides. Bring the photo and any saved tick.
