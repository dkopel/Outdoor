# Eye Injuries

## Foreign object on the surface
Don't rub. Rubbing grinds particles into the cornea and creates abrasions that take days to heal. Have the person try blinking and producing tears first — small dust often washes out on its own. If the object is visible on the white of the eye or under a lid, gently flush with clean drinking water for 5–10 minutes. Tilt the head so water runs from the inner corner outward, not into the other eye. Lift the upper eyelid and look up, down, left, right to inspect. If the object is on the cornea (the colored part), do not try to remove it — patch both eyes loosely to limit movement and evacuate.

## Chemical splash
Flush immediately. Use any clean water — bottle, stream, hydration bladder — and flush continuously for at least 20 minutes, longer for alkaline (lye, lime, cement) splashes. Keep the eye open with your fingers while flushing; the person's natural reaction is to clench shut. Pour from the inner corner outward to protect the other eye. Remove contact lenses if present after flushing has started, not before. Even after thorough flushing, evacuate to medical care — chemical burns can progress over hours.

## Snow blindness (UV keratitis)
Sunburned corneas, usually from a day on snow or high-altitude reflective surfaces without sunglasses. Symptoms appear 6–12 hours after exposure: intense pain, gritty "sand in the eye" sensation, redness, light sensitivity, sometimes temporary vision loss. There is no fast cure. Cover both eyes with a cool damp cloth, rest in a dark space, take an over-the-counter pain reliever the person normally uses, and wait 24–48 hours. Vision returns. Prevention: dark wraparound sunglasses or goggles whenever you're on snow, water, or above treeline.

## Blunt impact / penetrating injury
A blow to the eye (rock fall, branch, falling gear) needs medical evaluation if there's bleeding inside the eye (visible blood in the colored part), changes in vision, severe pain, or sudden flashes or floaters. For penetrating injuries — anything stuck in the eye, anything that broke through the surface — do not attempt to remove the object. Stabilize it with a cone of cardboard or tape around the object (not pressing on the eye), patch the uninjured eye too (eyes move together), and evacuate sitting up. Do not give food or water; surgery may be needed.

## Prevention
Eye protection is one of the cheapest, most effective pieces of outdoor gear and one of the most often left at home. Wear it for: chainsaw work, splitting wood, cooking near a fire, any high-altitude or snow travel, fishing (hooks fly back), and mountain biking. Sunglasses for UV, clear or amber safety glasses for impact. UV-rated goggles for high alpine. Spares matter — a scratched single pair on day one of a long trip is bad news.
