# Asthma Attack

## Recognize it
Asthma attack signs: wheezing on exhale (or both directions in severe attacks), shortness of breath, tight chest, persistent cough that won't quit, difficulty speaking in full sentences. Severe attack: silent chest (so little air moving you can't hear wheeze), inability to speak more than 1–2 words, blue lips, pulling in of skin between ribs or above collarbone with each breath, severe confusion. A "silent chest" is a critical sign — the airways are nearly closed.

## Triggers in the outdoors
Common backcountry asthma triggers: cold dry air (winter, high altitude), exercise (especially without warm-up), pollen and mold (spring/fall in forests), smoke from forest fires (huge concern in dry seasons in the western US), wildfire smoke from hundreds of miles away (regional air quality alerts matter), respiratory infections (common in groups), stress and fatigue, altitude itself (thinner air requires more breathing work).

## Treat with their rescue inhaler
The standard rescue inhaler is albuterol (Ventolin, ProAir) — a blue inhaler. Standard dose for an attack: 2 puffs, wait 60 seconds, 2 more puffs. Use a spacer if they have one (improves drug delivery). Wait 5–10 minutes; if symptoms improve, monitor. If not, repeat 2–4 puffs.

Severe attacks may need higher doses — up to 8 puffs of albuterol back-to-back, often repeated every 20 minutes for the first hour. This is more than the label dose; it's standard wilderness medicine for severe asthma when ER is hours away. If they have a peak flow meter, measure before and after to assess severity.

## What to do
1. Sit them upright — leaning slightly forward is most comfortable for breathing.
2. Help them use their rescue inhaler.
3. Calm them — anxiety worsens breathing difficulty.
4. Loosen tight clothing around the chest and neck.
5. Remove triggers if identifiable (move away from smoke, into shade or shelter).
6. If they have a written asthma action plan, follow it.
7. If symptoms don't improve in 10–15 minutes with inhaler use, evacuate as a high-priority emergency.

## When to evacuate immediately
- Severe attack signs above (silent chest, can't speak full sentences, blue lips).
- Rescue inhaler not relieving symptoms.
- Attack continues or worsens after 15–30 minutes of treatment.
- Severe agitation or sudden quietness in the patient.
- Loss of consciousness.

Use PLB, satellite SOS, run for help. Severe asthma can be fatal in hours.

## Field improvisations
A rescue inhaler is more effective with a spacer (a tube that holds the medication so it can be inhaled over several breaths). Field improvisation: cut a 2-liter water bottle in half, use the upper half as a spacer — inhaler in the cap end, mouth on the cut end. It's awkward but improves delivery substantially compared to direct inhaler use, especially for someone in distress.

## Pre-trip planning for known asthma
Anyone with known asthma should:
- Carry rescue inhaler AND a spare. Don't go into the backcountry with one inhaler; they fail.
- Carry their preventer/controller inhaler too — daily use, not for attacks.
- Pre-medicate before known trigger exposure (cold air, heavy exertion): 2 puffs of rescue inhaler 15 minutes before the trigger reduces attack risk.
- Have an asthma action plan written by your doctor — distribute to group members.
- Tell the group leader what you have and how to help.

## Exercise-induced asthma
Many people have asthma only triggered by exercise — they don't have an attack at rest but get one with sustained exertion. Strategies: warm up gradually for 10–15 minutes, breathe through the nose or a buff (warms air), avoid cold-air sprinting, pre-medicate with rescue inhaler. Some people manage backcountry exertion well with these techniques but need to know their limits.

## Wildfire smoke
Dangerous for asthmatics. Air quality apps and government AQI alerts make this manageable: above AQI 100, plan reduced activity; above 150, asthmatics should avoid outdoor exercise; above 200, asthmatics should seek indoor refuge if available. In remote backcountry, you can't always get out — pre-trip checking matters more, and having extra inhaler doses matters more.
