# Suspected Spinal Injury

## When to suspect
Mechanism matters as much as symptoms. Suspect spine injury after: any significant fall from height, a fall onto the head or neck, being struck by a moving object, motor-vehicle accidents, diving into shallow water, avalanche burial, lightning strike, near-drowning, or any blunt trauma above the collarbone. Symptoms suggesting spinal injury: neck or back pain, numbness or tingling, weakness in any limb, loss of bladder or bowel control, difficulty breathing, abnormal posture. When in doubt, treat as spinal until ruled out.

## Don't move them
Stabilization in place is the priority. Tell the person clearly to hold still. Have someone (you or another responder) maintain manual stabilization of the head — kneel at the head, hands on either side of the head/neck, hold gently in line with the body without pulling. Don't tilt, turn, or jostle. The natural neutral position is jaw and ears in line with the shoulders. Maintain manual stabilization continuously until medical help takes over or you must move them for safety.

## If you must move them
Only move a suspected spine-injury patient if their life is at risk where they are — drowning, fire, falling rocks, hypothermia in deteriorating weather. To move: use a log roll — three or more rescuers, one at the head maintaining alignment, others rolling the body together as a single unit while keeping the spine straight. The head rescuer calls "ready, roll" so everyone moves together. If you don't have help, you may need to drag them by the shoulders, supporting the head against your forearm.

## Airway concerns
If a suspected spine-injury patient stops breathing, you must protect the airway. Use a jaw-thrust maneuver (push the jaw forward without tilting the head) rather than the standard head-tilt-chin-lift. If you cannot maintain a clear airway with jaw-thrust, you may need to use head-tilt-chin-lift despite the spine risk — airway always takes priority over spine. An unconscious vomiting patient needs to be on their side; use a log roll to a side position.

## Evacuation
This is a high-priority evacuation: PLB, satellite SOS, calling 911. Stay with the patient. Treat for shock (cover with insulation, keep warm). Monitor breathing. Splint any obvious limb injuries without moving the spine. Receiving medical professionals will use a backboard, cervical collar, and head blocks — these are not for field improvisation by untrained responders. Do not attempt to put on a homemade neck collar; bad immobilization is worse than no immobilization.

## After it turns out OK
Most suspected spinal injuries turn out to be muscle strains, bruises, or shock — but you cannot tell without imaging. The right play is to treat as spine until proven otherwise. After a near-miss like a long fall onto a backpack, the person may feel fine for hours before pain develops; they should still be evaluated medically. Don't let "I feel OK now" override the mechanism of injury when deciding whether to evacuate.
