# Raccoons, Mice, and Other Small Raiders

## Raccoons
Raccoons are highly intelligent, have hands as dexterous as monkeys, and have learned that humans = food. They open coolers, untie food bags, manipulate latches, and work in pairs at popular campgrounds. They also carry roundworm, rabies, and distemper. Never feed raccoons, even tame-seeming ones. Store food in bear canisters, hard-sided coolers with latched lids, vehicles, or proper hangs. A raccoon scratching at your tent at night usually means food was inside or smelled of food — keep tent clean of scented items.

## Mice and rats
Rodents nest in shelters, raid food bags, and gnaw through gear and packs. They prefer warm dry spaces — leave pack pockets open at camp so a mouse exploring inside has an exit and doesn't chew through a closed zipper. Hang food bags from cord even in non-bear country to keep mice out. In some western US areas, deer mice carry hantavirus, which causes a rare but often fatal pulmonary syndrome — avoid disturbing rodent droppings in old cabins and outbuildings.

## Marmots and ground squirrels
In western mountain country, marmots chew through hiking boots, hydration hoses, and pack straps for the salt residue from sweat. Don't leave gear unattended outside in marmot areas. Store boots inside the tent at night. Be aware that habituated marmots near popular trailheads are bold; one will bite you for a granola bar in your hand. Plague is endemic in western ground squirrel populations — keep distance, especially in California, New Mexico, Arizona, and Colorado.

## Porcupines
Porcupines chew anything salty — boots, pack straps, sweat-soaked clothing, axe handles, wooden gear. The bigger issue is curious dogs that learn the hard way; quills are barbed and require pliers and professional removal. Keep dogs away from porcupines. Humans almost never get quilled unless they touch one; just give them distance. They're slow, hard to scare, and not aggressive.

## Skunks
Smell makes them famous; they also bite and carry rabies. A skunk approaching a camp wants food — secure food properly. Don't corner a skunk; back away slowly. Sprayed gear: tomato juice is myth, doesn't work. Mix of hydrogen peroxide + baking soda + dish soap is the actual effective treatment. Burn or pack out heavily-sprayed items. Dogs sprayed: bathe outside, repeat over days; eyes need flushing with water if directly hit.

## General small-mammal hygiene
Wash hands after handling pack contents that contacted unknown surfaces. Avoid touching face after camp tasks. Don't drink directly from any natural water that animals visit. Watch for unusual behavior in any mammal: rabies presents as friendly, disoriented, or aggressive animals that should be avoiding you. Report sick or injured wildlife to rangers rather than approaching. Don't try to help wildlife yourself — well-intentioned interventions usually go badly.
