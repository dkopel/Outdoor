# Telling a Black Bear From a Grizzly

Color is unreliable — black bears can be brown, blond, or cinnamon, and grizzlies range from blond to nearly black. Use body shape and face profile instead.

## Shoulders
Grizzlies have a prominent muscular hump above the shoulders. Black bears do not — their highest point is the rump when they walk.

## Face profile
Grizzlies have a dished (concave) face profile, with a forehead that drops sharply to the muzzle. Black bears have a straighter, more "Roman-nosed" profile from forehead to nose.

## Ears
Black bears have taller, more prominent, pointed ears. Grizzly ears are shorter, rounder, and look small for the head.

## Claws
Grizzly front claws are long (2–4 inches) and only slightly curved — visible from a distance and ideal for digging. Black bear claws are shorter (about 1–2 inches) and more strongly curved for tree climbing.

## Tracks
A grizzly's claw marks land well ahead of the toe pads in tracks. A line drawn across the top of the front-paw toe pads passes through the bottom half of the smallest toe. On a black bear, the same line passes through the top half of the smallest toe, and claw marks (if any) are closer to the toes.

## Why this matters
Defensive grizzly attacks and defensive black bear attacks require **opposite** responses (play dead vs. fight back). In practice, surprise encounters are chaotic and species identification can be uncertain. When unsure: deploy bear spray if available, do not run, talk calmly, and back away slowly. If attacked and unsure of species, follow the grizzly protocol (play dead) only if the attack feels defensive — surprise, sow-with-cubs, or carcass defense — and fight back hard if the attack is silent, sustained, or happens at your tent.
