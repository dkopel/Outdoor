# Copperheads

## Range and habitat
Copperheads inhabit the eastern and central US — from southern New England through Florida (excluding the tip), west to Texas and parts of the Midwest. They're the most common venomous snake bite in the eastern US (more bites than rattlesnakes), though their venom is the least potent of the major US pit vipers. They prefer mixed deciduous forest, rocky hillsides, swampy edges, and suburban yards with cover.

## Identification
Adults 2–3 feet. Coppery-tan to pale brown background color with distinct dark hourglass-shaped crossbands — narrow at the spine, wide on the sides (some say "Hershey Kiss" shaped). Head distinctly triangular, copper-colored on top giving the species its name. Pit between eye and nostril. Vertical (cat-like) pupils. Juveniles have a bright yellow tail tip used to lure prey — a useful field ID for young snakes.

## Behavior
Copperheads rely on camouflage and freeze when threatened — they don't usually rattle or flee. This is why they account for so many bites: people step on or near them without seeing them. They strike fast when finally provoked but usually inject less venom than larger pit vipers. Most bites are defensive after the snake was stepped on, sat on, or picked up.

## Venom and symptoms
Copperhead venom is hemotoxic — destroys tissue locally and disrupts blood clotting. Symptoms develop over 30 minutes to 2 hours: significant pain at the bite, swelling that progresses up the limb, bruising, sometimes tissue necrosis (skin death) around the bite, weakness, nausea. Deaths are rare in healthy adults but tissue damage and limb dysfunction can be severe. Children, elderly, and small pets are higher-risk.

## Field response
Same as for any pit viper bite (see wildlife.rattlesnakes):
- Move away from the snake.
- Sit or lie down, keep calm.
- Remove jewelry and tight clothing from the bitten area.
- Keep the limb at heart level.
- Mark the leading edge of swelling, note time, re-mark every 15 minutes.
- Photograph the snake from safe distance if possible — but do not try to capture.
- Evacuate to a hospital with antivenom (Crofab works for copperheads).
- Do NOT cut, suck, ice, apply tourniquet, give alcohol, or use electric shock.

## Avoiding bites
Watch where you step in eastern forests, especially around fallen logs, rock outcrops, and brush piles. Wear sturdy boots and long pants. Use a flashlight when walking at night in summer. Don't reach into wood piles or under porch steps without looking. Don't pick up "dead-looking" snakes — they often aren't. Most copperhead bites in the eastern US happen to people who handled or harassed the snake.

## Suburban encounters
Copperheads are very tolerant of human-modified habitats and often live in suburban yards. They eat mice, frogs, large insects. Their presence in a yard isn't unusual. Encourage them to leave by removing brush piles and wood piles near walking paths, keeping grass short, and patching gaps in foundations. Don't try to kill them — most bites in yards happen during attempted killings. State wildlife agencies have advice for relocation.
