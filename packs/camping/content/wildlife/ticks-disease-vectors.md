# Ticks and Mosquitoes as Disease Vectors

## Why they matter more than the bite
Mosquitoes are the deadliest animal on earth by direct count, mostly via malaria worldwide. In the US, mosquitoes spread West Nile virus, Eastern equine encephalitis, La Crosse, and locally-acquired Zika. Ticks spread Lyme disease (the most common vector-borne illness in the US), Rocky Mountain spotted fever, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan virus, and alpha-gal syndrome. The bite itself is minor — the disease risk is what makes prevention essential.

## Prevention: clothing and chemistry
The most effective combination: long pants tucked into socks, long-sleeve shirts, light colors so ticks are visible, plus permethrin-treated clothing (treats fabric, lasts through about 6 washes, kills ticks on contact). On exposed skin, EPA-registered repellents work: DEET (20–30%), picaridin (20%), oil of lemon eucalyptus (10–30%), IR3535. Apply repeatedly per the label. Reapply after sweating, swimming, or 4+ hours. DEET damages plastics and some synthetic fabrics; apply to skin, not gear.

## Tick checks
Daily, head-to-toe, especially after walking through brush. Common attachment sites: scalp, behind ears, neck, armpits, groin, waistband, behind knees, ankles. Use a mirror or have a partner check spots you can't see. Tumble-dry clothing on high heat for 10 minutes after a hike — heat kills ticks better than washing. Bathing within 2 hours of coming inside reduces transmission risk substantially because most disease transmission requires 24+ hours of attachment.

## Removing an attached tick
Use fine-tipped tweezers, grasp the tick as close to the skin as possible, pull straight up with steady firm pressure. Don't twist or jerk — mouth parts may break off and stay in the skin (these don't transmit disease but can cause local irritation). Clean the bite with soap and water or alcohol. Save the tick in a sealed bag with the date and location; if symptoms develop, the tick can be identified. Do not crush ticks with bare fingers — pathogens can enter through small skin breaks.

## When to see a doctor
Bullseye rash (expanding red ring with clearer center) is a classic Lyme sign — see a doctor immediately, often within 2–4 weeks of bite. Any of these symptoms in the weeks after a tick bite: unexplained fever, fatigue, joint pain, muscle aches, facial palsy (drooping on one side), severe headache. Many tick-borne diseases respond to early antibiotic treatment but worsen significantly if missed. Bringing the saved tick to the appointment helps. Don't wait for a "perfect" rash; treat early when in doubt.

## Mosquito-specific protection
Cover up at dawn and dusk when most species are most active. Sleep under a mesh head net or in a tent with sealed mesh, not a fly-only tarp. Sleeping pad and bag treated with permethrin discourages mosquitoes through the night. In areas with West Nile or EEE outbreaks, check the state health department's local advisories. People with severe reactions or known immune issues should be more aggressive about chemical repellent and netting.
