# Brown Recluse Spider

## Range — narrower than you'd think
Brown recluse populations are firmly established in the central south and central midwest US: Arkansas, Missouri, Kansas, Oklahoma, eastern Texas, southern Indiana, Illinois, Kentucky, Tennessee, Mississippi, Alabama, and adjacent states. Occasional individuals turn up outside this range, but established populations are rare in the Pacific Northwest, Northeast, or far Southwest. The vast majority of "brown recluse bites" diagnosed outside this range turn out to be MRSA infections, other spider bites, or unrelated skin conditions.

## Identification
Small spider, body 1/4 to 1/2 inch long, leg span up to 1.5 inches. Tan to dark brown, sometimes pale yellow-tan. The diagnostic feature is the violin-shaped marking on the back (cephalothorax), with the violin's "neck" pointing toward the abdomen. Six eyes arranged in three pairs (most spiders have eight) — this is more reliable than the violin mark, which can be faint in juveniles. Long thin legs. Web is small, irregular, usually in undisturbed indoor spaces.

## Where they live
Indoors, primarily — basements, attics, closets, behind furniture, in stored boxes, in shoes left undisturbed, between folded sheets. They prefer dry, dark, undisturbed spaces. Outdoors they hide under rocks, in woodpiles, in shed corners. They're not aggressive — bites happen when the spider is pressed against skin (putting on a shoe, getting into bed where a spider is between the sheets, reaching into a stored garment).

## The bite and what follows
The bite itself often goes unnoticed — many victims don't recall being bitten. Over the next hours: mild stinging, then increasing pain, redness, and swelling. Within 1–2 days, a classic "bullseye" lesion can develop: pale center surrounded by reddish ring, sometimes blistering. In severe cases (about 10% of bites), the central tissue becomes necrotic — black, sloughing skin that ulcerates over days to weeks. Some cases develop systemic symptoms: fever, chills, weakness, joint pain, blood disorders, kidney issues (loxoscelism).

## Treatment
Most brown recluse bites heal with simple care: wash, keep clean, monitor. For developing necrotic lesions: see a medical provider. There is no specific antidote. Treatment is wound care: debridement of dead tissue, antibiotics if secondary infection develops, dapsone in some cases for inflammatory response. Severe necrotic wounds can take weeks to months to heal and may leave significant scars; rarely, skin grafts are needed.

## Diagnostic skepticism
Studies have found that the great majority of "brown recluse bite" diagnoses outside the spider's range are actually MRSA, other skin infections, vasculitis, or other conditions. Even within the range, many supposed bites are misdiagnosed. If you have a worsening skin lesion and didn't see a spider, ask the doctor to also consider MRSA — antibiotic treatment for MRSA is very different from supportive care for spider bites.

## Avoiding bites
Shake out clothes, shoes, and bedding that have been undisturbed in known recluse range. Inspect stored items before putting hands inside. Wear gloves when moving boxes or working in basements/attics. Reduce indoor clutter to reduce hiding spaces. Don't let dirty clothes pile up in corners. In cabins or rustic shelters in recluse range, store gear in sealed bags.

## At medical care
Bring the spider if you killed it — accurate identification matters. Take photos of the developing lesion at intervals so the doctor can see progression. Note where the bite happened (geographic location matters for diagnosis). Be patient — most bites are not severe; the worst cases are uncommon. Antibiotic prescriptions for "spider bites" are sometimes inappropriate if the wound is actually inflammatory from the bite alone.
