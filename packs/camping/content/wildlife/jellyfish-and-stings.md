# Jellyfish and Marine Stings

## US species of concern
Most US jellyfish stings are painful but not dangerous. Notable exceptions:
- **Portuguese man o' war** (not technically a jellyfish but a siphonophore) — Gulf, southeast Atlantic, sometimes Pacific. Long blue tentacles, severe sting, occasional severe systemic symptoms.
- **Box jellyfish (sea wasp)** — Tropical Pacific (Hawaii, Guam), small species seasonally in Atlantic Gulf. Severe stings; some species are among the most venomous animals on earth.
- **Sea nettle** — Chesapeake Bay, mid-Atlantic, Pacific Northwest. Painful sting, rarely dangerous.
- **Lion's mane jellyfish** — Atlantic and Pacific cold waters. Large, painful sting, occasional severe reactions.
- **Moon jelly** — Worldwide, mild sting, often safe to touch the bell. Don't push it.

## How they sting
Jellyfish tentacles have stinging cells (nematocysts) that fire when triggered by contact or chemical stimulus. Dead or detached tentacles still sting — washed up tentacles on beaches sting bare feet. The toxin causes pain, redness, welts, sometimes systemic reactions in sensitive people or after large stings.

## Field treatment
1. **Get out of the water** without panicking — splashing or swimming dragging tentacles spreads the sting.
2. **Carefully remove visible tentacle pieces** — wear gloves, use tweezers, or scrape with the edge of a card. Do NOT rub with hands.
3. **Rinse with seawater** (NOT fresh water — fresh water can trigger more nematocysts to fire). Use ocean water or saline.
4. **Vinegar** is effective for some species (especially box jellyfish, Portuguese man o' war) — pour liberally over the sting. For others (lion's mane, moon), vinegar doesn't help much but won't make things worse.
5. **Hot water immersion** (as hot as can be tolerated, 110–113°F) for 30–90 minutes denatures jellyfish toxin and significantly reduces pain.
6. **Don't pee on it** — folk myth, doesn't work, may make things worse.
7. **Over-the-counter pain reliever** per label.

## When to evacuate
Severe systemic symptoms after a sting: difficulty breathing, chest pain, severe abdominal cramps, widespread hives, dizziness, confusion. Any sting near the eye or mouth, or covering a large area of the body. Children with significant stings. Any sting from box jellyfish (especially tropical species — these can be rapidly fatal). Pre-existing heart or breathing problems.

## Stingrays
Stingrays are not jellyfish, but the treatment overlaps. Stingrays lie partly buried in shallow sand; people step on them, and the barbed spine on the tail whips into the foot or ankle. Pain is severe, often described as the worst pain the victim has ever felt. The barb may break off in the wound.

**Treatment**: control bleeding with direct pressure. Submerge the wound in hot water (as hot as tolerated) for 30–90 minutes — this is by far the most effective immediate treatment for stingray pain. Remove the barb if visible and easy; leave deep barbs for medical removal. Evacuate to medical care — barb fragments inside cause infections, and stingray wounds frequently develop secondary infections.

## Avoiding stingrays — the shuffle
Shuffle your feet when wading in shallow sandy water. This pushes stingrays out of the sand before you step on them. Don't step down; slide forward. This single technique prevents almost all stingray injuries to swimmers and waders.

## Sharks
US shark attacks are vanishingly rare relative to ocean exposure — about 30–40 unprovoked attacks per year, mostly in Florida, plus occasional Hawaii, Carolinas, California. Avoid: swimming alone, swimming at dawn/dusk, swimming in murky water, swimming with shiny jewelry or bleeding cuts, swimming near fishing boats or schools of fish. If you spot a shark, calmly exit the water; don't splash. If attacked, fight back aggressively — hit eyes, gills, nose. Most attacks are exploratory bites; severe outcomes are rare.

## Sea urchins, coral, fire coral
Stepping on sea urchins in tide pools or reef areas: spines stick in the foot, often break off. Soak in hot vinegar to dissolve spine fragments, then remove with tweezers. Embedded fragments often work out over weeks. Don't grind tongue or hands on coral — many corals (fire coral especially) have stinging or abrasive surfaces. Use water shoes in any tidal/reef environment.
