# Spiders and Scorpions

## Medically significant US spiders
**Black widow** (Latrodectus species) — shiny black with red hourglass on the underside of the abdomen, common across the lower 48. Bite causes severe muscle cramps, especially in the abdomen, sometimes mistaken for appendicitis. Sweating, weakness, restlessness. Treatable; rarely fatal. Most bites happen when hands or feet are placed where spiders are hiding — woodpiles, outhouses, storage sheds.

**Brown recluse** (Loxosceles reclusa) — small, brown, with a violin-shaped marking on the back. Found in the central south and central midwest US (Texas, Arkansas, Missouri, Tennessee, etc.). Bite often unnoticed initially; necrotic (tissue-killing) wound develops over hours to days. Most bites heal slowly with conservative care; large necrotic lesions need medical care.

Most "spider bites" diagnosed by people are actually MRSA infections or other skin issues. Without seeing the spider, the diagnosis is uncertain. Painful red bumps are usually not spider bites in the US.

## US scorpions
The vast majority of US scorpions are not medically dangerous — painful sting like a wasp, no lasting harm. The exception: the Arizona bark scorpion (Centruroides sculpturatus) in the desert Southwest. Yellowish-brown, slender, about 3 inches. Sting can cause severe pain, numbness around the sting, sometimes muscle spasms or breathing trouble — especially in small children. Treatable with medical care; rarely fatal in healthy adults but warrants evacuation.

## Avoiding bites and stings
Shake out boots and clothing before putting them on, especially in desert or outbuilding camps. Don't reach into woodpiles, brush, holes, or dark corners without looking. Wear gloves when picking up logs or rocks. Sleep in tents with sealed mesh, with the door zipped — bark scorpions climb and have been found in shoes inside tents. Use a UV flashlight at night in desert country — scorpions fluoresce greenish-white under UV and become easy to spot and avoid.

## If bitten or stung
Wash the area with soap and water. Ice or cold compress for 10–20 minutes at a time for pain and swelling. Take an over-the-counter pain reliever the person normally uses. Watch for signs of serious envenomation: severe muscle cramps (suggesting black widow), spreading wound necrosis over days (brown recluse), or severe systemic symptoms (any). Adult healthy people typically don't need ER care for routine spider/scorpion encounters; children, older adults, and anyone with severe symptoms should be evaluated.

## When to evacuate
Evacuate immediately for: any signs of anaphylaxis (rare but possible), suspected bark-scorpion sting in a child, suspected black-widow bite with severe abdominal pain or muscle cramping, brown-recluse bite with rapidly spreading dark tissue, any spider/scorpion incident with shortness of breath or chest pain, any sting in eye or mouth. Photograph the spider/scorpion from a safe distance if possible; identification helps medical decisions but is not required for treatment.
