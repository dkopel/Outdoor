# Polar Bears

## A different bear
Polar bears are unlike other US bears in two critical ways: they are obligate carnivores (other bears are omnivores) and they actively hunt humans on rare occasions, considering us prey rather than threats to defend against. Polar bear attacks are rare but disproportionately fatal. Range: Arctic Alaska coast (Beaufort Sea, Chukchi Sea), occasional sightings on northern Alaska rivers and inland during ice-loss seasons.

## Where you might encounter them
Most travelers won't. Polar bears live on sea ice and along Arctic coastlines. Backpackers in ANWR, Gates of the Arctic NP, the North Slope, Kotzebue, Barrow/Utqiagvik, and small Arctic villages may encounter them. Hunters traveling Arctic rivers in late summer/fall increasingly run into bears displaced by ice loss. Polar bears occasionally swim long distances inland in fall.

## Climate-driven changes
Sea ice loss has changed polar bear behavior over recent decades. Bears now spend more time on land, are often thin and hungry, and travel further inland searching for food. Human-bear conflicts have increased in coastal Arctic villages. Encounters during fall on land are more common than they were 30 years ago.

## What polar bears do that other bears don't
Polar bears stalk. They may follow a person for hours from concealment, choose a moment to attack from behind, or wait at a campsite. Unlike grizzly or black bears, polar bears that approach humans are very rarely "defensive" — they're predatory. The standard advice for grizzlies (play dead in defensive attacks) DOES NOT apply to polar bears. Polar bears must be fought, deterred, or escaped.

## Active defense
**Don't try to outrun.** Polar bears sprint 25 mph and swim faster than humans.

**Bear spray works** on polar bears (multiple documented successful defenses). Carry it accessibly. Use at close range; don't waste it on a distant approaching bear.

**Firearms** — communities that live alongside polar bears almost universally carry rifles (12-gauge slug or .375+ caliber) on the land. Travelers in true polar bear country should consult with local guides on what to carry and how. This is a real exception to general bear country gun-vs-spray debates.

**Noisemakers, flares, gunshots into the air** sometimes work to deter approaching bears. Some Alaskan communities use bear-bangers (firework-like deterrents) regularly.

## Camping protocols
Travelers in real polar bear country either: (1) camp with a guard system (rotating watch), (2) use bear-deterrent fencing (electrified perimeter wire — actually effective), (3) sleep in fortified structures, or (4) accept that the encounter response is "wake up to a polar bear at your tent." Routine "hang your food" practice for black bear country is irrelevant — polar bears don't care about food storage; they care about prey availability.

## Get local guidance
If you're planning travel in polar bear country (ANWR, Arctic coast, North Slope), don't rely on this app. Talk to USFWS biologists, NPS rangers for that area, and local guides. Carry the proper deterrents (bear spray AND firearm appropriate to bear-capable caliber). Travel in groups. Camp inland when possible (bears patrol the coast). Know the recent bear sightings in your area before you start.

## Reporting and respect
Polar bears are a threatened species under the Endangered Species Act. Disturbance is a federal offense. If you must defend yourself, that's legal — but document everything (location, what happened, photos if possible) and report to USFWS or local authorities immediately afterward. The bear's ecological status doesn't change your right to survive an attack; the documentation matters for the legal record.
