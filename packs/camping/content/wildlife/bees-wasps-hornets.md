# Bees, Wasps, Hornets, Yellow Jackets

## Why this matters
Stinging insects kill more Americans every year than bears, mountain lions, and snakes combined — almost entirely through severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis). Even non-allergic people can be killed by mass stings from disturbed nests. Late summer and fall are peak risk as colonies are largest, food is scarce, and they're protective of nests. Sweet smells (sunscreen with fragrance, soda, fruit) attract them; dark clothing and quick movements provoke them.

## Avoiding stings
Watch where you step and reach — yellow jackets often nest in ground holes that look like nothing until you step on them. Be especially careful around log piles, wall holes, abandoned animal burrows, and overhanging eaves of shelters. Eat carefully outdoors — check food and drinks before each bite; a yellow jacket in your soda can sting your throat. Wear long sleeves and pants in known-active areas. Avoid wearing strong floral or sweet scents.

## If a single insect is near you
Do not swat. Move slowly away. Swatting often results in stings or releases alarm pheromones that summon more insects. Walk, don't run, away from any nest you've disturbed. If they pursue, get into water or dense brush; many species break off pursuit after 50–100 yards from their nest. Yellow jackets and Africanized honey bees ("killer bees") in the southwest can pursue far longer and are more aggressive.

## Removing a stinger
Honey bees leave their stinger and venom sac in the skin — remove it within seconds if possible. Scrape it sideways with a fingernail, knife back, or stiff card; do not pinch with tweezers, which squeezes more venom in. Wasps, hornets, and yellow jackets do not leave stingers (they can sting repeatedly). Wash the area with soap and water. Cold compress reduces pain and swelling. Over-the-counter antihistamine and pain reliever per label help for normal reactions.

## Mass stings
A disturbed underground yellow jacket nest or bee hive can produce dozens to hundreds of stings in seconds. Run — straight away, through brush if needed, ideally through tall grass or trees that may slow pursuing insects. Cover the face with arms or a shirt as you run. Get to a sealed shelter (vehicle, building, tent with zipped mesh). Once safe, count stings — anything above 10–20 stings, or any signs of systemic illness (vomiting, weakness, breathing trouble), needs medical evaluation even if the person isn't known to be allergic.

## Anaphylaxis after a sting
If a stung person develops swelling of the face/tongue/throat, breathing difficulty, hives spreading rapidly, dizziness, or loss of consciousness — this is anaphylaxis. Treat as a critical emergency: epinephrine auto-injector immediately if available (see first-aid.anaphylaxis), call/PLB for rescue, position them appropriately for their symptoms. Even with epinephrine, evacuate every anaphylactic patient; biphasic reactions can return hours later. Anyone with a known severe stinging-insect allergy should carry epinephrine in the field — there is no field workaround.
