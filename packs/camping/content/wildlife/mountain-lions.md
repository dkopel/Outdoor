# Mountain Lions

## How rare attacks are — and why it matters
Mountain lion attacks on humans are very rare; in a typical year there are only a handful in all of North America. But mountain lions are obligate ambush predators: when they do attack, they target the head and neck and aim to kill quickly. Behavior in any encounter should be the opposite of bear behavior — you want to look big, loud, and not-prey.

## If you spot a mountain lion
Stop. Pick up small children immediately. Group together with companions, raise your arms and any gear (poles, jackets) overhead, and make yourself look as large as possible. Maintain eye contact (unlike bears, where steady eye contact may be read as challenge — for lions you want them to know you see them). Speak firmly and loudly. Do not crouch, kneel, turn your back, or run.

## Back away slowly
Move sideways or backwards toward better terrain or other people. Do not turn your back. If the lion crouches, lays its ears back, or vocalizes (hissing, growling), it may be considering an attack — back yourself toward terrain that limits its approach angles (a tree at your back, a rock face).

## If a mountain lion attacks
Fight back, immediately and aggressively. Most successful self-defenses against mountain lions involve the victim striking the lion's face, eyes, and nose with whatever is available — rocks, sticks, a knife, fists, trekking poles. Try to stay on your feet — lions often kill by knocking prey down to access the neck. Do not play dead. Keep fighting until the lion disengages or you can retreat to safer terrain.

## Children and dogs
Mountain lions disproportionately target small humans and small to medium dogs. In lion country, do not let children run ahead on the trail, and keep them within arm's reach in low-visibility terrain. Keep dogs on leash and close — a dog can both attract a lion and trigger predatory chase. After any encounter, report it to the local land management agency; tracking encounters helps protect other visitors.
