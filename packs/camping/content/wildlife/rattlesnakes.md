# Rattlesnakes

## Range and species
Rattlesnakes (genera Crotalus and Sistrurus) inhabit every state in the lower 48 except Maine, Delaware, and possibly Rhode Island, plus parts of southern Canada and most of Mexico. The most clinically significant species:
- **Western diamondback** (C. atrox) — Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, into California and the southern Great Plains. Aggressive, common, large (up to 5 ft).
- **Eastern diamondback** (C. adamanteus) — Florida and the southeastern coastal plain. The largest US rattlesnake, up to 7 ft, with the largest venom yields.
- **Timber rattlesnake** (C. horridus) — Eastern US from southern New England to north Florida and west to eastern Texas. Generally docile; bites less common than range suggests.
- **Mojave rattlesnake** (C. scutulatus) — Southwest deserts. Particularly dangerous due to potent neurotoxic venom in some populations.
- **Prairie rattlesnake** (C. viridis) — Great Plains and Rocky Mountain foothills.
- **Sidewinder, pygmy rattler, massasauga**, and others fill out the genus across various habitats.

## How rattlesnakes hunt and react
Rattlesnakes are pit vipers — they detect heat and prey with infrared pits between eye and nostril. They ambush, striking when warmth-emitting prey passes close. Toward humans, they prefer to avoid — most rattlesnake encounters end with the snake retreating or freezing. They rattle when threatened, but not always; some bites happen with no warning sound. They strike at distances up to about 2/3 of their body length, very fast (under 0.5 seconds).

## When and where bites happen
Most rattlesnake bites in the US happen in summer, in the South and Southwest, on the hands and feet of people who deliberately interacted with the snake (handling, photographing closely, or trying to kill it) or who stepped without looking. Common scenarios: reaching into rocks or brush, climbing over logs without seeing the other side, walking into tall grass, putting hands or feet near unseen snakes in shade.

## Avoiding bites
Watch where you put your hands and feet. Wear sturdy boots and long pants in rattlesnake country. Tap brushy ground ahead with a trekking pole. Avoid walking at night in summer in dry country without a light. Don't step over logs without looking on the far side. Keep dogs on leash — dogs investigate snakes and get bitten on the face, leading to severe swelling and potential airway compromise.

## If bitten by a rattlesnake (pit viper)
- Move at least 20 feet away from the snake to avoid a second bite.
- Identify (color, pattern, photograph from distance) but do NOT try to capture or kill — the snake doesn't need to be brought in.
- Have the patient sit or lie down, calm and still. Movement spreads venom faster.
- Remove rings, watches, tight clothing from the bitten limb before swelling.
- Mark the leading edge of swelling on skin with a pen, and note the time. Re-mark every 15 minutes.
- Keep the bitten limb at heart level or slightly below.
- Get to a hospital that can administer antivenom (Crofab is the primary US pit viper antivenom) as fast as conditions allow.
- Do NOT cut, suck, apply ice, apply a tourniquet, give alcohol, or use electric shock — all are myths that worsen outcomes.

## Dry bites and severity variation
About 20–25% of rattlesnake bites are "dry" — the snake didn't inject venom. There's no way to know in the field, so all bites need medical evaluation. Severity also varies: smaller adults, elderly, and those with cardiac/immune issues are higher-risk; healthy adults often recover with antivenom and supportive care. Children and small dogs face higher per-pound venom load and are at higher risk.

## Bites on dogs
Rattlesnake-bitten dogs typically present with rapid swelling, sometimes drooling, weakness, and shock. Get to a vet immediately — many vet clinics in rattlesnake country stock antivenom. Rattlesnake vaccines exist for dogs in high-exposure areas; they may reduce severity but don't prevent bites.
