# Mosquitoes and Disease

## Why they kill more people than any other animal
Globally, mosquitoes cause more human deaths than any other animal — primarily through malaria. In the US, mosquito-borne diseases of concern include West Nile virus (most common, nationwide), Eastern equine encephalitis (rare, Northeast and Gulf Coast, very high mortality), La Crosse encephalitis (Upper Midwest, mostly children), Saint Louis encephalitis (East and South), Jamestown Canyon virus, Powassan virus (rare, severe), occasional Zika (transmitted locally in Florida, Texas), dengue (occasional Florida/Hawaii outbreaks), and Chikungunya (rare, southern states).

## When and where bites happen
US mosquitoes are most active dawn and dusk, with some species active all day in shade and during cloudy weather. Heavy populations near standing water — wetlands, drainage ditches, ponds, slow streams, snowmelt pools, swamps. Recent rain plus warmth = peak activity. Some areas have multiple peaks per season (early summer hatch then fall hatch).

## Prevention — what actually works
**Repellents on skin**: EPA-registered DEET (20–30% concentration), picaridin (20%), oil of lemon eucalyptus (10–30%), IR3535. DEET is the gold standard; picaridin works almost as well and doesn't damage plastics. Apply every 4 hours when active, after sweating, after swimming.

**Treated clothing**: permethrin-treated clothing kills mosquitoes that land on the fabric. Treatment lasts about 6 wash cycles. Pre-treated clothing (Insect Shield brand) lasts up to 70 washes. Treating your own clothing with permethrin spray costs ~$10 and protects multiple trips.

**Clothing coverage**: long sleeves, long pants tucked into socks, light colors (mosquitoes target dark moving targets). Loose-fitting fabric is harder for them to bite through than tight.

**Head nets**: essential for serious mosquito country (Alaska, Maine in spring, Canadian shield). Worth the few ounces of weight.

**Tent mesh**: sealed, intact mesh keeps mosquitoes out. Hammock camping needs an integrated or separate bug net for the same reason.

## What doesn't work well
Citronella candles, "bug zappers," ultrasonic repellents, Vitamin B1, garlic supplements, vodka-soaked clothes, dryer sheets, eating bananas, marigolds, sage — all popular but with mixed-to-no evidence behind them. Citronella sprays/lotions are mildly effective at very high concentrations. Most "natural" repellents are far less effective than DEET or picaridin.

## Recognizing a mosquito-borne illness
Most West Nile infections are asymptomatic, but a small percentage develop serious neuroinvasive disease — high fever, severe headache, stiff neck, confusion, weakness, sometimes paralysis. EEE has very high mortality and warrants immediate care. Symptoms generally appear 3–14 days after the infecting bite. Anyone returning from significant mosquito exposure with fever and severe headache should see a doctor and mention mosquito exposure.

## High-risk regions in the US
Florida, Gulf Coast, Lower Mississippi Valley (year-round risk). Upper Midwest (June–September). Northeast (June–September). Alaska in early summer (legendary mosquito numbers, though disease risk is lower). California Central Valley, parts of Arizona near water. Hawaii (some areas have dengue/Zika risk).

## Travel-specific
International travel to malaria zones: take pre-trip prophylaxis (chloroquine, doxycycline, atovaquone-proguanil, mefloquine — chosen by a travel medicine clinic). The CDC travel pages list current malaria/dengue/yellow fever risk by country. Some destinations require yellow fever vaccination certificate. Don't wing it on tropical disease prevention.
