# Bears

## Black bear vs grizzly (brown) bear
Black bears are common across most of North America, including the East Coast and Southeast. They are usually smaller (150–400 lb), tend to be more skittish around humans, and rarely act predatorily toward adults. Their face profile is straight, and they lack the prominent shoulder hump grizzlies have. Grizzly/brown bears live primarily in Alaska, western Canada, and parts of the northern Rockies (Wyoming, Montana, Idaho). They are larger (300–800+ lb), have a distinct shoulder hump, a dished face, and longer claws. Color is a poor identifier — black bears can be brown and grizzlies can be dark.

## Avoiding an encounter
Make noise as you hike, especially in dense brush, near loud water, or around blind corners. Bear bells are largely useless; voice (singing, talking, periodic "hey bear" calls) carries much further. Hike in groups when possible — bears almost always avoid groups of three or more. Store food and scented items properly (canister, hang, or bear box) at least 100 yards (90 m) from your sleeping area. Cook and eat at least that far from your tent. Never store food in your tent — ever, anywhere, in bear country.

## If you see a bear at a distance
Stop and assess. If the bear hasn't seen you, back away quietly the way you came and detour widely. If the bear has seen you, identify yourself as human: speak calmly, wave your arms slowly. Do not run; running triggers chase instinct in many predators and you cannot outrun a bear (35 mph short sprint). Pick up small children. Group up with companions. Back away slowly, never turning your back. Give the bear a way out — never corner one.

## Bear spray
Carry bear spray in a hip or chest holster where you can deploy it in seconds, not buried in your pack. Practice removing the safety. Bear spray is effective at close range (within 30 feet / 9 m) — wait until the bear is committed, then aim slightly downward at the bear's face from a stable two-handed grip and spray a 2-second burst directly into its path. Wind matters; sidestep to avoid the cloud blowing back into your own face. Bear spray is statistically more effective than firearms at stopping bear attacks.

## If a bear attacks
Defensive attack (the bear is surprised, protecting cubs or food): play dead. Drop to the ground face down, legs spread to make rolling harder, hands clasped over the neck, pack left on as armor. Stay still until the bear leaves the area. Move only when you're sure it's gone. Predatory attack (rare, but a black bear stalking you, no obvious provocation, returning after you tried to leave): fight back. Use bear spray, rocks, sticks, fists — focus blows on the face and nose. For grizzlies, fight only if the attack is clearly predatory or continues after playing dead has failed.
