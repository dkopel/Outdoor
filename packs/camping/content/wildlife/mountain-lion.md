# Mountain Lion (Cougar) Encounters

Mountain lions — also called cougars, pumas, panthers, and catamounts — are elusive and usually avoid people. Sightings most often happen at dawn or dusk. Most hikers will never see one even in lion country. If you do, the encounter requires the opposite response from a bear: appear large and aggressive, do not play dead.

## If you encounter a mountain lion
Do not run. Running can trigger the chase instinct, and a cougar is faster than any human. Stand and face the animal. Make eye contact. Do not crouch or bend over — crouching humans look like prey.

Make yourself appear as large as possible: raise your arms, open your jacket wide, hold a pack or trekking poles above your head. Move slowly to higher ground if you can.

Speak firmly and loudly. Do not scream or squeak; speak with deep, authoritative tones. Throw rocks, sticks, or anything within reach without bending down.

## Protecting children and pets
Pick up small children immediately without bending or turning your back. Hold them as high as possible. Keep pets on a short leash and pulled close to your body. A solo small person or a pet running ahead on a trail is a textbook target for a stalking cougar.

## If a mountain lion attacks
Fight back. Do not play dead. Successful defenders have used trekking poles, rocks, hiking sticks, jackets, garden tools, and bare hands. Concentrate strikes on the eyes, nose, and throat. Stay on your feet — if knocked down, protect your neck and head with your arms and keep fighting.

## Reducing the risk
- Hike in groups when possible. Cougars almost exclusively attack lone people.
- Avoid hiking alone at dawn, dusk, or night in known lion habitat.
- Keep children close and within arm's reach on trails.
- Make noise around blind corners and dense brush.

Report all mountain lion sightings to park rangers. Report any encounter where the animal did not flee immediately — that may indicate a habituated or food-conditioned lion that requires management response.
