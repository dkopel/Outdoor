# Bear Encounter — What To Do

## If you see a bear at a distance
Stop and stay calm. Most bears will avoid you if they hear you coming. Maintain distance — the National Park Service recommends staying at least 100 yards (about 91 m) from black and grizzly bears. Use binoculars or a telephoto lens to observe. If the bear has not seen you, quietly back away the way you came and take an alternate route. Always leave the bear an obvious escape route.

## If a bear has seen you
Do not run. Running can trigger a chase response, and bears can sprint as fast as a racehorse uphill or down. Identify yourself by talking calmly in a low voice so the bear recognizes you as a human, not prey. Wave your arms slowly and stand your ground. Make yourself look as large as possible; move to higher ground if it is safe to do so. Keep your pack on — it protects your back and neck. Do not drop food: rewarding a bear with food trains it to approach humans and can lead to its eventual killing.

## If the bear approaches
Continue speaking calmly but firmly. Do not scream, squeal, or imitate bear sounds — high-pitched noises can sound like prey. If you have bear spray, ready it. Move sideways slowly, not backward; this lets you watch the bear and avoid tripping. If you have small children, pick them up immediately without bending or turning your back.

## Special caution: mothers with cubs
A grizzly sow defending cubs is the most dangerous encounter scenario. Never place yourself between a mother and her cubs, and never approach cubs even if no adult is visible. Back away immediately and give the family group as much space as the terrain allows.

## Never
Do not climb a tree — both black bears and grizzlies climb. Do not run. Do not surrender your pack or food. Do not approach for a photo.

This is general guidance only. Local park rangers have the most current information about bear activity in your area; check with a visitor center before heading into the backcountry.
