# Venomous Snake Bite — Field Response

A venomous snakebite is a medical emergency. Definitive treatment requires antivenom administered at a hospital. Field first aid is about getting the patient there alive and minimizing tissue damage on the way.

## Get away from the snake
Move the patient at least 20 feet away from where the bite occurred. Do not try to capture, kill, or photograph the snake at the risk of a second bite. Even a dead snake or a decapitated head can still envenomate by reflex.

## What to do
1. Sit or lie the patient down. Keep them calm — fear and exertion speed venom spread.
2. Remove rings, watches, and constrictive clothing from the bitten limb before it swells.
3. Position the bitten limb at roughly heart level — neither elevated above nor far below.
4. If possible, wash the bite gently with soap and water; cover with a clean, dry bandage.
5. Mark the leading edge of swelling or redness on the skin with a pen and write the time next to it. Re-mark every 15–30 minutes during evacuation so the receiving hospital can see the progression.
6. Begin evacuation to professional medical care immediately. Do not let the patient walk if they can be carried — physical exertion accelerates venom spread.
7. If you can call 911 or activate a personal locator beacon (PLB) or satellite communicator, do so.

## What NOT to do
The CDC is explicit: many traditional remedies are harmful.
- Do **not** apply a tourniquet.
- Do **not** cut or slash the bite.
- Do **not** try to suck out the venom — by mouth or with an extractor device. Extractors do not work and can worsen tissue damage.
- Do **not** apply ice or immerse the wound in cold water.
- Do **not** apply electric shock.
- Do **not** give the patient alcohol, aspirin, ibuprofen, or other NSAID pain relievers — they can worsen bleeding. Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is acceptable.
- Do **not** wait for symptoms to develop before deciding to evacuate. Symptoms can have a delayed onset.

## Watch for envenomation signs
Symptoms include severe pain, rapid swelling, bruising at the bite, nausea, vomiting, weakness, sweating, blurred vision, difficulty breathing, and changes in heart rhythm. Some bites are "dry" (no venom injected) but you cannot assume this in the field — treat every venomous-snake bite as envenomated until proven otherwise at a hospital.

This is general first aid guidance. Always seek professional emergency care for any suspected venomous snakebite.
