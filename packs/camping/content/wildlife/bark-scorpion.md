# Arizona Bark Scorpion

## The only medically dangerous US scorpion
Out of roughly 90 scorpion species in the US, the Arizona bark scorpion is the only one whose sting routinely causes severe systemic symptoms in healthy adults and is occasionally fatal. Range: Arizona (especially central and southern), parts of New Mexico, southern Utah, far western Texas, southeastern California, and northern Mexico. Common in suburban Phoenix and Tucson — many residents have stung in their own homes.

## Identification
Slender yellow-tan to pale brown body, 2.5–3 inches long including tail. Pincers thin and elongated (unlike most US scorpions, which have thick chunky pincers). Tail held high and curled forward when active. Climbs surprisingly well — found on walls, ceilings, in trees, and on bark (giving the species its name). Most US scorpion species are larger, darker, and ground-dwelling; the bark scorpion is the unusual climber. Fluoresces bright greenish-white under UV light.

## Behavior
Nocturnal. Hunts insects, especially crickets, at night. Hides during the day under rocks, in cracks, in shoes, in folded clothing, in bedding. Climbs trees and walls — bark scorpions in houses are often on ceilings or walls, dropping onto sleepers. Doesn't intentionally seek humans but stings defensively when pressed or stepped on.

## Symptoms of sting
Initial: intense local pain, often with no swelling or visible mark (unusual — most scorpion stings show clear swelling). Numbness around the sting site, sometimes spreading. Severe envenomations (more common in children): roving eye movements, twitching muscles, slurred speech, drooling, agitation, frothy saliva, sometimes seizures, occasionally respiratory difficulty. Symptoms develop over 30 minutes to a few hours and can persist 24–48 hours.

## Children and severe cases
Children under 6 are at much higher risk for severe systemic symptoms. A bark scorpion sting on a small child can be a true emergency. Symptoms in kids: sudden inability to sit still, abnormal eye movements, drooling, sometimes vomiting, occasionally seizures. Healthy adults typically have intense local pain but milder systemic symptoms.

## Treatment
Wash the sting area. Cool compress for pain. For mild cases in healthy adults: rest, OTC pain reliever per label, monitor. Evacuate to medical care for: any child under 6 with sting symptoms, any sting with rapid onset of severe systemic symptoms (eye movements, muscle twitching, breathing changes), elderly with sting, pregnancy, immunocompromised. Antivenom (Anascorp) is available in Arizona hospitals and dramatically reduces symptom duration in severe cases.

## Avoiding stings
Shake out clothing, shoes, and bedding before use, especially if you've been in known scorpion country. Tuck pant legs into boots. Don't walk barefoot at night in or near homes in Arizona. Use a UV flashlight at night to scan walls, floors, and outdoor areas — bark scorpions glow bright green-white. Seal cracks in baseboards and around doors. Reduce insect populations (they're scorpion food); remove debris that attracts insects.

## Camping in scorpion country
Sleep in tents with sealed mesh and zipped doors. Inspect inside the tent and shoes before bed. Don't lay sleeping bags directly on the ground; use a pad and check before getting in. Bring a UV flashlight on Arizona desert trips — bark scorpions hunt at night and are far easier to spot than to find with a regular light. If anyone in the group is small or has underlying conditions, talk pre-trip about how an emergency evacuation would happen.
