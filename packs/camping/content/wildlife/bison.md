# Bison

## The most dangerous animal in Yellowstone
Bison injure more park visitors than any other animal in Yellowstone — far more than bears, mountain lions, or wolves combined. They look slow and placid; they're not. A bison can reach 35 mph in short bursts, weighs 1,000–2,000 pounds, and can pivot to gore from a near-stop. Most injuries happen to people who approached for photos, walked too close to a herd, or got between a cow and a calf.

## Range
Wild bison herds: Yellowstone NP, Grand Teton NP, Wind Cave NP, Custer State Park (SD), Theodore Roosevelt NP (ND), Badlands NP, Wood Buffalo NP (Canada). Smaller managed herds in many state and federal wildlife refuges. Also wild bison and elk in some Plains states (Antelope Island UT, Henry Mountains UT). Plus various private and commercial herds.

## Distance and behavior
NPS guidance: stay at least 25 yards (23 m) from bison and elk, 100 yards (90 m) from bears and wolves. If a bison stops grazing and watches you, you're too close. If it raises its tail, paws the ground, snorts, lowers its head, or starts walking toward you — back away immediately. These signs precede a charge by seconds.

## Bison charges
Once a bison commits to a charge, you have very little time. Get behind something solid — a vehicle, a large tree, a boulder. Bison charging lines are mostly straight forward, so running 90° to the side is more effective than running directly away. Don't try to climb a small tree — bison will knock it over. Don't run toward open ground. Don't try to outrun it head-on; you can't.

## If gored
Bison gore with their horns and toss victims with their massive heads. Injuries are often severe: deep punctures, broken ribs, internal organ damage, spinal injuries. The bison usually moves on after the initial attack but may circle back. Get to cover and stay still until the bison has truly left. Treat for shock, control bleeding, splint suspected fractures, evacuate emergent (PLB, ranger station, vehicle).

## In a vehicle
Bison on roads in Yellowstone is a daily traffic phenomenon. Stay in your vehicle. Drive slowly past — if the herd is on the road, wait it out; don't push through. Don't honk to clear them; agitated bison can charge a vehicle (and sometimes damage it). If a bison approaches the car, stay inside with windows up. Don't lean out for photos.

## Calves and protective cows
Late spring through summer, cow bison are highly protective of calves. Walking between a cow and a calf will trigger a charge. Hikers crossing meadows with scattered bison need to know whether calves are present and route widely around any close mother-calf pair. From late summer through fall, bull bison are in rut and aggressive; the safest practice is to give all bison wide berth year-round.

## The selfie problem
Most bison injuries in parks happen to visitors who approached too close for photos. Tourists from areas without dangerous wildlife consistently misjudge bison behavior — they look like big cows, but they aren't. Rangers and signs repeat the 25-yard rule constantly because people keep ignoring it. Use a telephoto lens. Pull off the road in a designated turnout. Never approach for selfies. Every season, social media circulates footage of someone getting tossed by a bison they were six feet from.
