# Coral Snakes

## A different venom family
Coral snakes are the only elapids native to the US — same family as cobras and mambas, with neurotoxic venom that paralyzes muscles. This is different from rattlesnakes/copperheads/cottonmouths (pit vipers with hemotoxic venom). Coral snake bites are uncommon (the snakes are secretive and have small mouths) but historically dangerous; the US Coralmyn antivenom is no longer manufactured, and supplies are limited.

## Range and species
**Eastern coral snake** (Micrurus fulvius) — Coastal plain from North Carolina through Florida and west to Louisiana. Most encountered species.
**Texas coral snake** (M. tener) — Texas, Arkansas, Louisiana. Similar venom and behavior.
**Sonoran coral snake** (M. euryxanthus) — Arizona and New Mexico. Smaller, less venom per bite.

## Identification — "red touches yellow"
The classic North American mnemonic: **"Red touches yellow, kill a fellow. Red touches black, friend of Jack."** Coral snakes have red, yellow, and black bands in repeating order with RED touching YELLOW. Harmless mimics (king snakes, scarlet kingsnake) have red touching BLACK. This rule only works for US coral snakes north of Mexico — in Central and South America, mimics break the pattern.

Other features: small head not much wider than body (unlike pit vipers' triangular head), small eyes with round pupils, rings encircle the entire body (not just dorsal patches), 18–30 inches long, slender. Black snout (sometimes red bands extend onto the head).

## Behavior
Coral snakes are secretive and reluctant to bite — they're often found under leaf litter, logs, or in sandy soil. Most bites happen when people pick them up (sometimes mistaking them for harmless king snakes) or accidentally grab them while moving leaf litter or rocks. The snake's mouth is small and the fangs are short and fixed; bites usually involve chewing rather than a quick strike. This means bites typically require sustained contact — relatively avoidable if you don't handle them.

## Venom and symptoms
Neurotoxic, blocks neuromuscular junctions. Onset is delayed — sometimes 6–12 hours after bite, then progresses rapidly. Symptoms: drooping eyelids, double vision, slurred speech, weakness progressing to paralysis, difficulty swallowing and breathing. There may be little local pain or swelling at the bite — DON'T be reassured by an unimpressive-looking bite. Respiratory failure is the cause of death.

## Field response
Any suspected coral snake bite is a medical emergency, regardless of how the bite looks. Symptoms may be delayed but progress fast once they start. Evacuate to a hospital with neuromuscular support capability (mechanical ventilation) as fast as possible. The traditional pit-viper "pressure immobilization" technique (firm wrap from distal to proximal of the bitten limb) is sometimes recommended for coral snake / elapid bites — unlike pit viper bites, where it's contraindicated. Field practice is uncertain; the best move is fast evacuation and hospital care.

## Antivenom complications
The historic US Coralmyn antivenom is in limited supply, with some hospitals having expired stock and others having none. Some hospitals use Mexican-manufactured antivenom (Coralmyn or Antivipmyn-Tri) imported under FDA special-use protocols. The decision to use antivenom is made by the receiving physician. Even without antivenom, supportive care (especially mechanical ventilation through paralysis) saves most bite victims.

## Avoiding bites
Don't pick up any snake you can't ID with 100% certainty. The red-touches-yellow rule is reliable in the US but only if you can see the bands clearly. In good light, a coral snake is unmistakable. In poor light or rushed, mistakes happen. Wear sturdy boots and gloves when working in leaf litter or under cover in coral snake range. Watch toddlers in southeastern yards — coral snakes occur in suburban gardens and small bright-colored objects attract small children.
