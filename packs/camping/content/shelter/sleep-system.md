# Your Sleep System

## The pad is half the system
Body heat moves into cold ground faster than into cold air. A sleeping bag rated to 20°F is useless without an insulating pad, because compression under your weight pushes the down/fill flat. Sleeping pads are rated by R-value: R-2 for warm summer, R-3 to R-4 for 3-season, R-5 or higher for shoulder season and winter. Below freezing, two pads stacked (a closed-cell foam beneath an inflatable) provides redundancy if one fails and adds R-value.

## Sleeping bag basics
Temperature ratings on sleeping bags are notoriously optimistic. Two main systems: EN/ISO comfort rating (the temperature an average woman sleeps comfortably; closer to what cold sleepers feel) and limit rating (what an average man can survive but not sleep well). Use comfort rating as your real planning number. Down fills are lighter and pack smaller but lose loft when wet; synthetic fills are heavier but insulate when damp. Hydrophobic-treated down sits in between.

## Bag construction
Mummy bags (tapered, narrow foot box, hood) are warmest per ounce because they hug the body. Quilts (no zipper, no back insulation) are popular ultralight choices but only work paired with a high-R-value pad and a tight cinch around the neck. Rectangular bags are heavier and colder, useful for car camping or warm conditions. Hoods matter: untold body heat exits the head; sleep with a hood up, drawn tight, in real cold.

## Layering inside a bag
For colder-than-rated nights: dry base layers, dry socks, warm hat. Sleeping bag liner adds 5–15°F depending on fabric (silk and merino are top choices). Tomorrow's clothes flat on top of your bag as additional insulation. Don't sleep in tomorrow's wet outer layers — they don't dry inside your bag, and damp clothing wicks warmth. Don't sleep in too many layers either: a sleeping bag works by warming a layer of air around you; over-stuffing the bag with clothes reduces loft and warmth.

## Site selection for sleeping warm
Pick a campsite out of wind. Avoid low spots where cold air pools at night (valley bottoms can be 10°F colder than benches 50 feet above). Avoid pitching on ice, snow patches, or directly on cold rock without thick foam beneath. Tents on snow benefit from a packed/leveled platform — fresh powder under a tent acts as cold air pockets. Position your tent so the lowest, smallest end faces wind.

## Eating and drinking for warmth
Eat a substantial dinner with fat content (cheese, nut butter, oil-rich foods) — digestion produces internal heat through the night. Drink warm liquid before bed (caffeine in moderation; too much keeps you up and makes you pee). Pee before bed — your body wastes energy keeping urine warm. Pee bottle with secure cap saves a midnight trip in cold or rain. Snack on a few calories if you wake up cold; the body needs fuel to generate heat.
