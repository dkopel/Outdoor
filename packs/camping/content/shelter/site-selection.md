# Choosing a Safe Campsite

Picking a campsite is a safety decision more than a comfort decision. Most bad nights in the backcountry trace back to a poor site choice made when people were too tired or hurried to look properly. Spend the extra ten minutes.

## Look up — widow-makers
Before you anything else, look at every tree above your intended sleeping area. Look for:
- **Dead standing trees** (snags) — these fall without warning, even on a calm night.
- **Large dead branches** in live trees ("widow-makers") that could come down in wind.
- **Trees leaning at strange angles** with exposed roots.
- **Loose bark, mushrooms, or rot** on trunks.

If you see any of the above, move. A snag falls maybe once a decade — but it only takes once. Wind storms knock down dozens overnight.

## Avoid drainages and low spots
- **Dry stream beds** can become flash-flood channels in storms 5 miles away. Never camp in a wash, especially in desert canyons.
- **Drainage bottoms** collect cold air at night (the "cold air pool"), making them 10–15°F colder than a slight rise above.
- **Wet ground or seeps**: condensation soaks your gear and tent floor.
- **Below treeline saddles and passes** are wind tunnels — find a sheltered spot just off the saddle.

## Avoid the very top, too
- **Ridge tops and summits** are exposed to wind, lightning, and weather. Descend at least a few hundred feet before camping if a storm is possible.
- **Open meadows**: beautiful, but exposed and prone to dew condensation.

## Water — close but not too close
- Camp at least **200 feet** from any lake or stream (about 70 paces). This is a Leave No Trace standard, protects water quality, and gets you away from animal trails to water.
- Listen to the water before you decide — heavy rapids drown out other sounds, including bears or your tent neighbor in trouble.

## Wildlife corridors
- Avoid camping on a game trail. Animals will use it at night.
- Avoid camping near berry patches, salt licks, or carcasses.
- In bear country, sleep at least 100 feet (some areas require 200) upwind from your kitchen and food storage. The "bear triangle" places sleeping site, cooking site, and food storage at three corners.

## Surface
- **Flat, well-drained, durable**: rock, gravel, sand, or compacted soil. Avoid vegetation when alternatives exist.
- **No protruding roots or rocks** where your hips and shoulders will land.
- **Slope**: a slight (1–3°) slope is fine and actually drains better. Sleep with your head uphill. Avoid significant sidehill — you slide off your pad all night.

## Other practical considerations
- Sunrise direction: a campsite that catches first light dries dew off your tent fast.
- Wind direction: orient the tent so the strongest stake-out points face into the prevailing wind.
- Privacy from other campers (LNT principle 7).
- Existing impacted sites: in popular areas, use them rather than creating new ones. In pristine areas, spread use so no site becomes impacted.

If something feels wrong about a site — your gut is reading the terrain. Move.
