# Bivy Sacks

## What a bivy is and isn't
A bivy sack is a weatherproof shell that goes over your sleeping bag, adding wind and rain protection without a tent. Modern bivies are typically waterproof-breathable (eVent, Gore-Tex, similar) on top and tougher waterproof fabric on the bottom. They range from minimal (Outdoor Research Helium) to small one-person tents (Mountain Hardwear Conduit). A bivy is not a tent: it's coffin-tight, condenses moisture in cold/humid weather, and offers no living space for cooking, dressing, or waiting out a multi-day storm.

## When a bivy makes sense
Solo trips where weight matters more than comfort. Alpine climbs where you need to sleep on small ledges. Fast-and-light trips where you're moving every day. As a backup when a tarp alone might not be enough. Pre-dawn alpine starts where you want minimum setup/teardown time. Emergency shelter alongside your ten essentials. As a footprint or vestibule extension under a tarp.

## Bivy + tarp combination
The most versatile lightweight shelter system: a small tarp pitched a foot or two over a bivy. Bivy keeps you dry from below and gives windproof bug protection; tarp keeps rain off and provides usable living space for putting on boots, cooking, organizing gear. Pitched low and tight, this combination handles heavy weather while weighing under 2 pounds total. Common on long thru-hikes and ultralight setups.

## Condensation reality
A breathable bivy still produces condensation in cold weather — your body emits about 1 liter of water vapor overnight. The bag inside the bivy gets damp, sometimes very damp, by morning. Strategies: leave the head opening cracked, sleep with the bag drawn closed but not zipped fully, air the bag dry every morning when possible, choose a sleeping bag with water-resistant down or synthetic for bivy use. Wet down loses loft and warmth fast; synthetic bags handle damp better.

## Emergency bivies
A "space blanket bivy" — silver-coated mylar in body-bag shape — weighs about 4 ounces and packs to a fist. It's not breathable, so condensation soaks you, but it provides genuine wind protection and reflective heat retention for an unplanned overnight. Worth carrying as part of the ten essentials. Better are heavyweight "all-weather blankets" (3M, AMK Heatsheets) that are more durable. These won't save your trip but might save your life in a forced bivouac.

## Practical use
Pre-deploy your bivy with the foot end downhill in a slight depression — gravity helps you stay put. Drape outerwear over the chest area to add insulation. Pad rocks under your hips with extra clothing or a sit pad. In rain, drape pack contents in dry bags inside the bivy with you; the bivy alone is barely big enough for a sleeping bag, let alone gear. Always have a way to get a hand to the zipper from inside; in panic, fumbling for the exit makes the situation worse.
