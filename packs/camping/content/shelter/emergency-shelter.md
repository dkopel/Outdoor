# Emergency Shelter Without a Tent or Tarp

## Choose a smart spot first
Spend 5 minutes finding a good spot before you spend an hour building. Good shelter sites are: out of wind, away from cold-air sinks (low spots), away from widow-makers (dead trees and branches), near building materials but not in active wildlife travel corridors. Existing features — a windfall log, a thick conifer, a rock overhang — save enormous work. Don't camp in dry creek bottoms (flash flood risk) or under big snow loads on branches above you.

## Debris hut (no insulation, lots of leaves)
The debris hut is a one-person survival shelter built from a ridge stick and piled debris. Find or place a strong ridgepole at chest height, supported by a tree fork or two stakes — long enough to crawl into and slope down to ground at the foot end. Lean smaller branches against the ridgepole at a 45–60° angle on both sides, then pile leaves, pine needles, and forest duff 2–3 feet thick over the entire frame. Crawl in feet-first and pull a "door plug" of debris in after you. Properly built, it traps body heat well below freezing.

## Snow shelters
A "quinzhee" is the most accessible snow shelter: pile snow into a dome about 7 feet across and 5 feet high, let it settle (the snow grains bond) for at least an hour, then hollow it out from a doorway at one end. Walls should end up ~12 inches thick. Always poke a ventilation hole near the top — carbon dioxide accumulates fast in enclosed snow space. A snow trench works in shallow snow: dig a body-length trench, cover with branches and then snow. Both shelters benefit from a sleeping pad — never put your body directly on snow.

## When time matters more than comfort
If conditions are deteriorating fast, focus on getting out of wind and rain first, perfection later. Crawl under the lowest branches of a thick conifer and you'll find a surprisingly dry pocket — sweep needles into a thick bed, sit on your pack with a foil blanket wrapped around you, and you have an emergency shelter in under 5 minutes. The hours saved compared to building from scratch can mean the difference between waiting out a storm and getting hypothermic during construction.
