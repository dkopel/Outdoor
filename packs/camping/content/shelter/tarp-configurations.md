# Tarp Configurations

## A-frame (symmetric ridgeline)
The A-frame is the most weather-resistant general-purpose tarp pitch. Run a ridgeline rope between two trees about a body length apart, drape the tarp over it, and stake out the four corners at roughly 45° angles. Tighten guylines so the fabric is drum-tight — slack tarp flaps and leaks. The ends are open by default, so orient the long axis with the wind direction (open ends downwind/upwind, not crosswind). Good for rain protection with airflow.

## Lean-to (fire-friendly)
A lean-to uses one edge of the tarp as a high front opening, sloping the back down to the ground. Two short stakes hold the back edge to the dirt; two trekking poles or stout sticks raise the front edge. The geometry creates a single open face that you can pair with a low fire reflected by a log wall behind it. Good for cold-weather camps in tree-cover, but leaks if rain is driving sideways into the open face — orient the back into the prevailing wind.

## Plow point (single anchor, fast pitch)
For a minimal one-person shelter, anchor one corner of the tarp at the top of a tree or trekking pole, then stake the diagonally opposite corner down to the ground. Stake the two remaining corners outward to spread the fabric. This gives a tight, low-profile bivvy shape in two minutes; you sleep under the high corner. Good for solo emergency pitches when speed matters more than space.

## Site selection
Avoid camping under dead branches or whole dead trees ("widow-makers") — they kill people every year. Pitch on slightly raised ground so water flows around you, not through. Never dig drainage trenches around a shelter in undeveloped land — modern practice is Leave No Trace, and picking a better spot is the right answer. Watch the sky and wind before you pitch: orient the back of any open-faced shelter into the worst predicted weather.
