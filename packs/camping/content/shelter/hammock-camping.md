# Hammock Camping

## Why hammock
Hammocks let you camp where tents can't — rocky ground, slopes, swampy terrain. Lighter than tent + pad + footprint for many setups. You sleep off the ground, away from puddles and crawling insects. The trade-offs: you need two anchor points (trees) the right distance apart; you must insulate underneath you against cold air; setup is its own skill; you're more visible (and more vulnerable) above ground in stormy weather.

## The basic setup
A modern hammock setup is: hammock body, tree straps (1-inch webbing, never bare cord on bark), bug net (integrated or separate), tarp pitched over the top, ridgeline (structural or non-structural), insulation under and over you. Tree straps spread the load across bark — narrow cord causes tree damage and is prohibited in many parks. Aim for a 30° hang angle measured at the strap: too flat is uncomfortable and stresses gear; too steep cocoons you.

## Insulation under you
The single most-failed lesson of hammock camping: your sleeping bag compresses under you and provides almost no insulation. You need an underquilt (hung under the hammock body, not compressed) or a closed-cell foam pad inside the hammock. Without it, you'll be cold below 65–70°F even with an excellent sleeping bag. Sub-freezing hammock camping requires a real underquilt rated for the conditions — this is not optional.

## Tarp pitching
Pitch the tarp first if rain looks possible, then set up the hammock under it. Use a separate ridgeline at chest-height between the trees and clip the tarp to it; that lets you remove the hammock without unpitching the tarp. Orient the tarp's long axis to deflect prevailing wind. Door-style tarps with doors closed protect from rain blowing sideways. For winter hammocking, full-coverage 4-season tarps with built-in doors create a wind-protected pocket.

## Site selection
Tree distance: 10–15 feet apart is typical. Trees thicker than ~6 inches at chest height; avoid leaning trees, dead trees, trees with cracked bark or visible rot. Look up: no widow-makers above you. Slope between trees doesn't matter (hammock hangs level). Ground beneath you should still be reasonable — if you fall out, that's where you land. Avoid pitching over active animal trails or wet areas mosquitoes love. Pack out everything.

## Failure modes
Strap pulling out of bark (loose strap, narrow strap, dead tree limb). Tree falling. Tarp blow-off in storm (under-tensioned guylines). Knot slipping (use proper hardware or knots that won't slip). Falling out (loose suspension or unfamiliar with motion). Cold underside (insulation gap). Practice the full setup at home, in your yard, before relying on it in the backcountry.
