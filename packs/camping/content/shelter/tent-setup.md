# How to Set Up a Tent

## How to pitch a tent step by step
The standard order for setting up most freestanding backpacking tents:

1. **Pick a flat spot** clear of sharp objects, away from widow-makers (dead branches and trees), and not in a low spot where water would pool.
2. **Lay down the footprint** (the ground sheet that protects the tent floor) — make sure it doesn't extend past the tent edges, or rain runs onto it and pools underneath.
3. **Unfold the tent body** on top of the footprint, doors facing where you want them — usually away from the prevailing wind.
4. **Assemble the poles** by snapping the shock-corded sections together. Most modern poles are color-coded or marked.
5. **Slide poles through sleeves or clip the body to poles.** Pop the poles into the grommets at each corner — the tent body now stands up.
6. **Stake out the four corners** of the tent floor — taut so the floor is flat and wrinkle-free.
7. **Drape the rainfly** over the top, doors aligned with the body's doors. The fly should not touch the inner mesh — that's where water wicks through.
8. **Clip and tension the rainfly** at each corner. Adjust until the fly stands clear of the body all around.
9. **Stake and tension the guylines** — most tents have 4–8. The tent's full weather performance depends on tensioned guys.
10. **Check the inside** for any drooping spots — adjust tension until it's drum-tight.

A practiced person can set up a 2-person backpacking tent in 5–10 minutes. The first time you pitch a new tent, do it in your backyard, not on day one of a trip.

## How to choose a tent site
Clear the spot of sharp sticks, pine cones, and rocks — anything larger than a marble will be a stone in your back after a few hours. Look up: dead branches above you can fall in wind ("widow-makers"). Look down for slope: pitching with your head slightly uphill is far more comfortable than the reverse, and even a 5° slope lets cold air pool around your feet. Stay 200 feet (60 meters) from water sources where regulations require — it's both leave-no-trace and avoids surprise high water in storms. Avoid drainage paths and depressions where rain collects. In bear country, pitch your tent at least 100 yards from your cooking area.

## Staking and guying the tent
Stake out the floor first, getting the corners taut and the floor flat with no wrinkles. Then erect poles and clip the body to them — most modern tents have color-coded poles or clips. Finally, attach and tension the rainfly. The fly should not touch the inner tent body anywhere — that's where rain wicks through. Guy out the fly attachment points so the fabric stands away from the body: most tents perform poorly until guylines are deployed and tensioned. In sandy or loose soil, use longer sand-stakes or weight stakes down with rocks. In hard ground, use a small rock as a hammer; don't break stakes by stepping on them.

## How to pitch a tent in heavy wind
If wind is up, orient the lowest, smallest end of the tent into the wind — usually the foot end. Use every guyline the tent has, double up stakes for the most stressed corners, and tension all guys equally so the fabric doesn't develop weak points. Avoid pitching in saddles between hills (wind channels), the crests of ridges (full exposure), or under big trees that may shed branches. In sustained high winds, valleys and timber are friends. If you arrive at camp and conditions look severe, consider relocating to a sheltered spot even at the cost of a few extra minutes' walking.

## Taking the tent down
Shake out the fly before stuffing — wet fabric in a wet stuff sack grows mildew fast. Disassemble poles by pushing from the center rather than pulling at the ends, which stresses internal shock cord. Wipe stakes clean. Pull stakes by lifting straight out, never sideways. If the tent is wet, pitch it later to dry rather than packing wet for more than 24 hours. Stuff bag works as well as fold — the tent flexes randomly each time and is less prone to creating permanent fold lines.

## Common mistakes
Pitching too loose — slack fabric flaps in wind, leaks rain, condenses moisture. Pitching on a slope without orienting head-uphill. Putting the rainfly on backward (look at the labeled door arrows). Skipping the guylines because it's calm at bedtime — wind picks up at 2 AM. Leaving the door zipped only partially — bugs find the gap. Pitching on bare ground next to camp neighbors' loud spots when you could move 50 feet for quiet.
