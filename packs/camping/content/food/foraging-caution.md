# Foraging Caution — Do Not Eat What You Cannot Confidently Identify

This app will not tell you what is safe to eat from the wild. The reason is simple: a mistake is potentially fatal, identification requires hands-on training and regional knowledge, and AI text descriptions are not a substitute for an experienced human eye on the actual plant.

## Why this is a hard line
- **Mushrooms** in particular are deadly to misidentify. The "destroying angel" (*Amanita virosa*) and "death cap" (*Amanita phalloides*) look like edible button mushrooms to non-experts. Symptoms appear 6–24 hours after eating, by which time liver damage is severe and often fatal. There is no reliable test for "safe vs. poisonous" by smell, taste, or appearance.
- **Berries**: many toxic species look like edible relatives (e.g., baneberry vs. elderberry). Birds eating a berry is not evidence it is safe for humans.
- **Roots**: water hemlock (the most toxic plant in North America) is in the parsley family and easily mistaken for edible relatives.
- **Greens**: foxglove looks like comfrey; deadly nightshade looks like various salad greens; pokeweed is toxic until thoroughly cooked.

## Folk tests do not work
- **The "universal edibility test"** taught in old survival manuals (sequential skin / lip / tongue testing) has been repeatedly debunked. Many deadly toxins (amanitin, cicutoxin) cause no immediate reaction. By the time symptoms appear, the dose is in your bloodstream.
- **"Animals eat it, so it's safe"**: false. Many animals metabolize toxins that kill humans. Squirrels eat amanitas with no harm.
- **"Bitter means poisonous"**: false. Many deadly plants taste bland. Some safe greens are bitter.
- **"Cooking destroys all poisons"**: false. Cooking destroys some toxins (pokeweed, common bean lectin), but not many others (amanitin, cardiac glycosides, cyanogenic compounds in some seeds).

## What you can rely on
- **Plants you have learned hands-on** from an experienced forager in your specific region, **multiple times across seasons**, until identification is confident at a glance.
- **Multiple identifying features cross-checked** against a regional field guide. Never identify on one feature.
- **Mushroom identification** specifically — bring suspect specimens to a local mycological society for verification before any consumption. Most major U.S. cities have one.

## In a survival situation
Modern wilderness search-and-rescue resources mean genuine survival foraging is almost never the right answer. A healthy adult can fast for many days. Hypothermia, dehydration, injury, and signaling failure cause survival deaths — almost never starvation. If you are lost, prioritize:
1. Shelter and warmth
2. Water
3. Signaling for rescue
4. Conserving energy

Foraging — even attempting to forage — burns calories you cannot afford and risks poisoning that turns a rescuable situation into a fatal one. Sit, signal, and wait.

## Things you can sometimes eat (low risk, with caveats)
A few wild plants are hard to confuse and historically used as starvation foods. Even these are not free of risk and should not be consumed casually:
- **Cattail rhizomes** (in clean water — accumulates pollution)
- **Inner bark of pines** (in cambium layer, edible in spring)
- **Acorns** (after extensive leaching to remove tannins)

This is mentioned for completeness, not as a recommendation. **Learn from a person, not an app.**

If someone in your group eats something wild and develops symptoms (nausea, abdominal pain, dizziness, confusion, vision changes), activate emergency rescue immediately and try to save a sample of what they ate.
