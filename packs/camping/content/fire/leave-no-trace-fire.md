# Leave No Trace Fire

Campfires leave the most lasting damage of any common backcountry activity: blackened rocks, sterilized soil, deadwood-stripped forests, and — in worst cases — wildfire. The Leave No Trace ethic does not say "no fires." It says: have one only when conditions allow, do it in a way that leaves the site indistinguishable from your arrival, and always prefer a stove.

## Use a stove
For cooking, a lightweight stove is faster, more reliable, and far lower-impact than a fire. In dry conditions and at high elevation where wood is scarce, a fire may be ethically wrong even when legal. Bring a stove on every trip.

## Where fires are appropriate
- In an **established fire ring** in a frontcountry or popular backcountry campsite.
- In a **mound fire** or **fire pan** in pristine areas (advanced LNT technique — see below).
- Below treeline where adequate dead-and-down wood exists.
- When fire is **legal and unrestricted** (check the park or forest's current fire-danger status — bans are common in summer).
- When wind, weather, and your skill all support full control.

## Where fires are wrong
- Above treeline where wood takes a century to regenerate.
- In any fire-restricted area (a violation regardless of conditions).
- In high fire danger (red flag warnings, drought, wind).
- In bedrock or sandstone country where heat scars are permanent.
- When you cannot stay awake to attend the fire.
- In small pristine sites where no impact yet exists.

## Wood selection — "small enough to break by hand"
Use only **dead and down** wood. Burn pieces small enough to break by hand — a stick thicker than your wrist takes too long to burn down to ash. Do not break branches off live trees; do not cut standing dead trees (snags) — they are habitat. Gather wood from a wide area, not all from around the fire site. Leave the immediate camp area looking foraged-but-not-stripped.

## How to leave no trace
1. **Use an existing fire ring** where one exists. Don't build a new ring.
2. In pristine areas: build a **mound fire** on a tarp covered with 6–8 inches of mineral soil. The fire sits on the mound, the heat does not reach the ground. After cooling, return the soil and scatter the tarp.
3. Burn all wood and coals **completely to ash**. Big charred chunks left behind are the LNT failure most visible from a quarter mile away.
4. **Drown the fire** with water. Stir the ashes. Add more water. Touch them with bare hands to confirm they're cold — that is the rangers' standard. If you can't touch it, it can still start a wildfire.
5. **Scatter cool ashes** widely, away from camp, well outside the fire ring or mound.
6. In a mound fire, **dismantle the mound** completely. Return the soil to where you took it. Brush the area to disguise the impact.
7. **Pack out everything that didn't burn**: foil, plastic, food scraps, fishing line.

## In high fire danger
Most national parks and forests restrict or completely ban fires during dry seasons. Check the current order before you leave the trailhead. Violating a fire ban is a federal misdemeanor and can cost you tens of thousands of dollars in suppression costs if a fire escapes. The right move in high fire danger is always: skip the fire, use the stove, sit closer together.
