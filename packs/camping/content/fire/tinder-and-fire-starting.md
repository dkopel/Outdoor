# Tinder, Kindling, and Fire-Starting Methods

Getting a fire lit is mostly about preparation, not technique. The single biggest cause of fire failure is impatient gathering: too little tinder, kindling too thick, fuel too damp.

## The three sizes
A fire needs three increasing sizes of dry combustible material, gathered and sorted **before** you light anything.

- **Tinder**: thin, fluffy, fast-burning material that catches a spark or flame instantly. Examples: dry grass tops, the inner bark of cedar/birch/poplar, milkweed fluff, fatwood shavings, dry pine needles, fine birch bark curls, and char cloth. You need a fist-sized bird's-nest of tinder for one fire.
- **Kindling**: pencil-thin to thumb-thick dry sticks, processed dry from the inside of larger pieces if needed. You need a double-armload.
- **Fuel**: thumb-thick to wrist-thick branches. Gather a triple-armload before you light. Running out for more once the fire is burning is the second most common failure mode.

Everything must be dry. Damp tinder lights briefly and dies; damp kindling sucks the heat out of the tinder and the fire collapses.

## Carry purpose-made tinder
Always carry one or two reliable tinder sources that don't depend on field conditions:
- **Petroleum-jelly cotton balls** in a small ziplock — burn 60+ seconds, light from one spark, cheap.
- **Fatwood / pitch wood** — resinous pine heartwood, lights easily and burns hot.
- **Commercial fire starters** (Wetfire cubes, Vaseline cubes, "fire ribbon").
- **Dryer lint** in a tin (works dry; useless wet).

In a backcountry emergency, one reliable tinder source can save a life that improvised damp leaves cannot.

## Ignition sources, ranked
1. **Lighter** (Bic-style butane): cheap, reliable, easy. Carry two in separate places. Limitations: fail at high altitude (low pressure) and in extreme cold.
2. **Stormproof matches**: burn in wind and even briefly underwater. Keep them in a waterproof container.
3. **Ferrocerium rod ("ferro rod")**: shave shavings off with the back of your knife, then strike to throw sparks. Works wet, never runs out of fuel, but requires good tinder and practice. The backup-to-the-backup.
4. **Magnifying glass**: works only in strong sun. A field expedient, not a primary.
5. **Bow drill / hand drill (friction fire)**: a survival skill that takes hours of practice to make reliable. Don't rely on it.

Carry at least **two** independent ignition sources, stored in different places. If your only lighter is in your pack and your pack falls in the river, you have a problem.

## The lighting sequence
1. Build the lay (teepee or log cabin) **with kindling pre-stacked** around the tinder bird's-nest.
2. Strike the spark or flame into the tinder. Once it catches, blow gently and steadily on the base of the flame to feed oxygen.
3. As the tinder catches kindling, leave it alone — do not poke or rearrange. Fires die from too much fiddling.
4. Add small kindling one piece at a time, then thumb-thick fuel. Wait for each layer to catch before adding the next.

## Adapting to wet conditions
Process larger wood: split sticks lengthwise with a knife or rock to expose dry inside wood. Make a "feather stick" — fine curls of dry wood still attached to the stick — for tinder. Use more tinder than you think you need. Stack the wet outer wood near the fire to dry while smaller dry stuff burns. See the wet-conditions fire chunk for more.

Patience and prep beat technique. Spend twenty minutes gathering before you strike a spark.
