# Starting a Fire in Wet Conditions

## Find dry tinder where rain didn't reach
After rain, almost everything is wet — except: inner bark of large standing dead trees, the underside of large overhanging branches, dead twigs still attached to standing dead trees (not lying on wet ground), pitch wood from old stumps and roots of conifers, paper birch bark (waterproof oils protect it even in deluges), and dry grass tucked under the lee of large rocks or fallen logs. Walk a wide circle before you give up on natural tinder. Carry waterproof commercial tinder (Vaseline-soaked cotton balls in a small bag) as insurance.

## Process larger wood for dry inside
Wet wood is usually only wet on the outside. Split larger pieces with a knife or hatchet and you'll reach dry wood within a half-inch. Feather sticks — long thin shavings carved into a stick still attached to the parent — give you a dense bundle of fine fuel that lights from a small flame. Practice making feather sticks before a wet trip; under a dripping tarp at dusk is not the time to learn.

## Build a platform off wet ground
Lay 2–3 wrist-thick branches side by side as a fire base, then build your tinder, kindling, and lay on top. The platform keeps your fire off the cold wet ground that would otherwise wick away heat. Find a fire spot under a tarp or under a thick conifer's lower branches where rain isn't directly hitting. A small flame that survives the first 10 minutes will dry out enough surrounding wood to keep itself going.

## Be patient and feed it
A wet fire takes time. Don't pile fuel on early — keep airflow and let small kindling fully ignite before adding the next size up. Use a small steady breath at the base of the flame rather than gusts. Once the fire is producing real coals, you can dry out larger wet pieces by stacking them around the fire (not on top) to drive moisture off before they're needed. If after 20 minutes of effort the fire isn't catching, you're missing dry tinder; go find more rather than burning all your matches.
