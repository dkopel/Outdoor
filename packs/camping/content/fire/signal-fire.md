# Signal Fires

## The international distress pattern
Three fires arranged in a triangle, evenly spaced, is the recognized backcountry signal for distress. Three of anything is the international distress call: three whistle blasts, three flares, three smoke columns. If rescuers are in the air looking for you, three fires in a clear triangle pattern is unmistakable. One fire could be a campfire; three together is "we need help here." If you only have one fire's worth of fuel, build one — but try for three.

## Visibility and smoke
Daytime: thick white smoke against dark forest, or thick dark smoke against snow or sand. Add green vegetation (pine boughs, wet leaves) to a hot fire to produce white smoke. Add tire rubber, plastic, motor oil (in an emergency) to produce black smoke. Burning a small amount of motor oil on a rag in an emergency makes a long visible column. Nighttime: bright flame matters, not smoke. Open flame in a clear spot is most visible from the air at night.

## Site selection
Open clearings on hilltops or ridges are most visible. Avoid signal fires under canopy where smoke disperses through leaves. River bars, beaches, and meadows work well. Build away from your shelter so rescuers don't land in your camp. Pre-arrange the three fires before you need them; once you hear or see aircraft, you may have only 60–90 seconds to ignite all three.

## Pre-built ready-to-light fires
Stack a tinder bundle, kindling, and small fuel pieces in each fire position. Cover with bark, rocks, or a flat piece of wood to keep dry. When needed: pull off the cover, light the tinder. Each fire should be self-sustaining within 30 seconds of ignition. Test the system in good weather before relying on it in an emergency. Carry waterproof matches or a ferro rod dedicated to signal fires, separate from cooking ignition.

## When to light
Light signal fires when: you hear aircraft, you see distant searchers, daylight is fading (rescuers stop air searches at sunset in most cases — try to be visible during late afternoon), or you're staying in place expecting rescue. Don't burn through your fuel ahead of search activity unless conditions are deteriorating. If you've activated a PLB, the response time is usually 6–24 hours; pre-build fires but don't light until you hear engines.

## Combine with other signals
Signal fires work alongside other signals, not in isolation. Bright-colored fabric (rain jacket, sleeping bag) spread on open ground or in trees doubles your visibility. Signal mirror works against any clear-sky sun. Whistle blasts repeating in threes catch ear from miles. PLB / satellite messenger is the most reliable rescue signal — pair fires with electronic signaling, not as a replacement. Survival is signaling consistently and visibly until rescue, not heroic singular efforts.
