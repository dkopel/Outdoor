# Backpacking Stoves

## Stove types and trade-offs
**Canister stoves** (Jetboil, MSR Pocket Rocket, etc.) burn pressurized propane/butane blend. Fast boil, simple to use, no priming. Performance drops sharply below ~25°F and at high altitude as pressure falls. Canisters can't be weighed for remaining fuel without a scale; partial canisters are a planning headache. Best for 3-season trips.

**Liquid-fuel stoves** (MSR WhisperLite, Dragonfly, etc.) burn white gas/Coleman fuel, sometimes kerosene or unleaded. Excellent performance in cold and at altitude. Refillable, so fuel quantity is exact. Heavier, requires priming (lighting a small pool of fuel to preheat the burner), needs occasional maintenance. Best for winter, expeditions, international travel where canisters are scarce.

**Alcohol stoves** (homemade or commercial like Trangia) burn denatured alcohol or HEET-brand fuel-line antifreeze. Very simple, no moving parts, near-silent. Slow boil (5–10 minutes vs canister 3–4). Limited to mild conditions; doesn't work well in wind or cold. Ultralight; popular for thru-hikers.

**Wood stoves** (Solo Stove, BushBox) burn twigs and small branches. Renewable fuel, satisfying, no fuel to carry. Forbidden during fire bans; slow; needs dry fuel; leaves ash mess. Backup or fire-band-flexible option.

## Safety basics
Never cook inside a tent or vestibule. Carbon monoxide from any stove can build to fatal levels in minutes in enclosed space. Snow shelters, hammock tarps, and even cracked-open tent vestibules are not adequate ventilation. The exception is brief water heating with intent and constant attention in a fully-open vestibule — but the safer practice is to step outside. Burns from tipping stoves and pots inside tents kill people every year.

## Fuel handling
Store liquid fuel and canisters away from heat sources. Refill liquid-fuel stoves outside, on a stable surface, with the stove cool, away from any flame. Check canister threads for damage before each use; a leaking canister can erupt in fire. Old or damaged canisters are not airline-allowed; check policies if flying to your trip. Recycle empty canisters per local rules — many outdoor stores accept them.

## Cold weather and altitude
For canisters in cold weather: keep canister inside your jacket between uses, sleep with it in your sleeping bag, run the burner low so the canister doesn't ice over. Use an isobutane-propane mix labeled for cold conditions, not pure butane. At high altitude, all stoves boil water at lower temperatures — food takes longer to cook even at a rolling boil. Account for that with extra fuel and patience.

## Cooking technique
Use a wind screen — even a stove rated efficient loses 50%+ of fuel to wind. Lid your pot to retain heat. Bring water just to a boil for dehydrated meals; you don't need a rolling boil for most rehydration. Cook simple meals in the field — anything complex burns or boils over. Always have a backup ignition source (matches in a waterproof container + a lighter; not one or the other).
