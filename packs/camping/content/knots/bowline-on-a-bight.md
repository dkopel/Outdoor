# Bowline on a Bight

## What it does
The bowline on a bight creates two parallel loops in the middle of a rope, without using the ends. Used for: rescue rigging (one loop under each arm of a patient lifted vertically, or one loop for each leg in an improvised harness), making a fast two-point anchor, hauling an unconscious person up a slope. It's a fundamental rescue and improvisation knot.

## How to tie it
1. Take a bight (folded loop) in the middle of the rope.
2. Make a basic bowline shape with the bight: form a small loop in the standing line, pass the doubled bight tip up through the small loop ("rabbit out of hole").
3. Open the doubled bight tip into a large loop, pass it around the entire knot ("around the tree"), then back down through the original small loop ("back into the hole").
4. Dress and tighten. Result: two parallel loops emerging from the knot, standing line both ways.

This is more complex than the basic bowline; practice 10 times before you need it. Many climbers find it helpful to first tie a regular bowline mentally, then "double up" the strands.

## Use in rescue
A casualty has fallen and needs to be raised vertically. You can tie a bowline on a bight at the appropriate spot on the haul rope, slip one loop under each of the casualty's armpits (one loop in front, one in back over shoulders), and haul. The two loops distribute the load across the chest. Add a chest harness or improvised wrap to stabilize.

## Improvised seat harness
Loop one of the bowline-on-a-bight loops around each of the casualty's thighs (legs through loops, knot at waist). Add a chest loop with separate webbing to prevent flip-over. The result is an improvised full-body harness from a single piece of rope. Less comfortable than a real harness; sufficient for short-duration rescue lowering.

## Use as a two-point anchor
A bowline on a bight tied between two equalized anchor points gives you two clip-in loops at the master point. The two loops can be clipped together or separately depending on need. Easier to untie than equivalent figure-eight setups.

## Strength and reliability
Bowline on a bight retains good rope strength (60–70% range). Like all bowlines, it can shake loose under cyclic loading if not backed up — add a barrel knot (double overhand) on each leg of the loop as a backup if the application warrants. Less prone to capsizing than a regular bowline because the doubled rope is held tight in the loop structure.

## Cousins and variants
- **Spanish bowline** — similar two-loop knot, more complex to tie, useful for some specific rescue scenarios.
- **Portuguese bowline** — two loops of different size.
- **Bowline on a coil** — historic ship-line knot, mostly obsolete.

For most outdoor and rescue use, the standard bowline on a bight as described above covers the need. Add the Spanish bowline only if you take a formal SAR course that teaches it.

## When NOT to use
Don't use any bowline variant for life-safety climbing tie-in — use a figure-eight follow-through. The figure-eight is harder to tie wrong and stays tied under repeated loading. Bowlines have specific advantages (easier to untie after loading, less bulk) that matter in rescue but not in routine climbing.
