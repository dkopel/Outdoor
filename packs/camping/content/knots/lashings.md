# Lashings — Joining Sticks and Poles

## What lashings do
Lashings tie two pieces of wood together rigidly to build a structure. Useful for: emergency shelter frames, drying racks, hand-rails, splints, signal towers, simple repairs to broken poles, river-crossing rafts in groups. The four primary lashings are square (for crossing pieces at right angles), diagonal (for crossing pieces under pull, like an X-brace), shear (for joining parallel pieces side-by-side), and round (binding parallel poles end-to-end).

## Square lashing — for right-angle joints
1. Start with a clove hitch around the vertical piece, just below where the horizontal will sit. Tuck the short tail under the wraps as you continue.
2. Bring the cord up and over the horizontal piece, around behind the vertical, back up and over the horizontal on the other side. Keep tension on each pass.
3. Make 3 full wraps in this pattern. Each wrap should sit inside the previous one, not on top.
4. Add 2 "frapping" wraps that go around the wraps themselves (not the wood) to cinch them tight.
5. Finish with a clove hitch on the horizontal piece. Tuck the tail.

A well-tied square lashing is rigid like the joint was nailed.

## Diagonal lashing — for X-braces and pulling joints
Use when the two pieces don't sit firmly at 90° (they want to pull apart at the crossing). Start with a timber hitch around both pieces at the crossing, pulled tight. Make 3 wraps in one diagonal direction across the crossing, then 3 wraps in the other diagonal. Add 2 frapping wraps between the pieces, around the wraps. Finish with a clove hitch on one piece. Diagonal lashings pull crossed pieces tight to each other; square lashings hold them at right angles.

## Shear lashing — for joining parallel poles
To make a longer pole from two shorter ones, or to make A-frame shear legs: lay the two poles parallel and overlapping. Tie a clove hitch around one pole, make 7–8 loose wraps around both poles together, add 2 frapping wraps between the poles to tighten the assembly, finish with a clove hitch on the other pole. To create A-frame legs, after lashing, swing the bottom ends apart to open them; the lashing acts as the hinge.

## Practical use in camp
Drying rack: two tripod legs, a horizontal piece lashed across with square lashings. Pot hanger: two vertical sticks driven into the ground, a horizontal lashed across, your pot hangs from a piece of cord over the horizontal. Bear bag pole: two vertical poles lashed in shear at the top, leaning, with your bag hung from the upper end (useful where trees are inadequate). Emergency stretcher: two long poles, cross pieces square-lashed at intervals, fabric stretched across (a wool shirt or jacket sleeves slid over the poles works).

## Material notes
Natural cord (jute, manila) bites into wood and stays tight; synthetic cord (paracord) is slicker and may need an extra wrap or two. Wet cord shrinks, tightening the lashing as it dries — sometimes useful, sometimes a problem if pieces shift. Sharp ends of the poles can cut cord; pad with cloth or wear gloves while working. Practice all four lashings at home before relying on them; they're a learnable skill but not instinctive.
