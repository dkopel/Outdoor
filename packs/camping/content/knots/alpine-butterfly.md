# Alpine Butterfly

## What it does
The alpine butterfly creates a strong, secure loop in the middle of a rope without using the ends. It's the standard knot for the middle climber on a glacier rope team, for isolating a damaged section of rope, for clipping to a fixed point mid-line, and for any application where you need a loop that won't slip when loaded from either end of the rope. Strong, low-bulk, and easy to untie even after heavy loading.

## How to tie it (twist method)
1. Hold the rope horizontally with both hands, about 18 inches apart.
2. Take the section between your hands and twist it twice — same direction both twists — to form a figure-eight shape with two loops side by side.
3. Pull the upper loop down behind the standing line, then up through the lower loop.
4. Pull on the two standing ends to tighten. The new loop is the alpine butterfly.

## How to tie it (hand-wrap method)
1. Lay the rope across your palm with one end hanging on each side.
2. Wrap the rope around your hand three times, going back to front each wrap.
3. Take the wrap closest to your fingertips (the topmost wrap) and pull it over the other two and under your hand.
4. Slide the knot off your hand and pull both standing ends to dress.

Both methods produce the same knot; pick whichever feels natural. Most climbers learn one and stick with it.

## Use in glacier travel
The middle of a glacier rope team is the most likely place for someone to fall into a crevasse, and the rope must hold that load while still letting the other team members anchor in. An alpine butterfly on each middle climber's harness gives them a clip-in point, and the knot does not slip when loaded from the side (toward the harness) as well as both ends.

## Use as a damaged-rope workaround
If your rope is damaged at a known point (sheath core hit, abrasion, cut), tie an alpine butterfly so the damaged section is inside the loop, isolating it. The rope can still be loaded from both ends, but the damaged section doesn't bear the climbing load. This is a temporary measure — retire the rope at the end of the day; don't trust an isolated damaged section for ongoing use.

## Strength and reliability
The alpine butterfly retains about 60–70% of rope strength, comparable to most other climbing knots (knots universally weaken ropes; this is expected). It's symmetric — loads from both ends behave identically. It doesn't capsize or roll. It's significantly easier to untie after heavy loading than a figure-eight on a bight. Climbers and rescue professionals consistently rate it among the most useful knots in their kit.

## Common variations and confusions
A "lineman's loop" is closely related and sometimes confused — slightly different geometry, comparable strength, also used for mid-rope loops. Some climbers tie what they call an "alpine butterfly" that's actually a lineman's loop; for nearly all practical purposes the difference doesn't matter. The important properties (symmetric, easy to untie, strong) hold for both.
