# Prusik Hitch

The prusik is a friction hitch — a small loop of thinner cord tied around a thicker rope. When weighted, it grips the main rope. When unweighted, it slides freely. The prusik is the foundational tool for ascending a rope, self-rescue, hauling a load, and dozens of other rope-work tasks. Every backcountry traveler near steep terrain or crevassed glaciers should know how to tie one and carry the cord to do it.

## What you need
- A length of 6mm or 7mm cord, approximately 4–5 feet long, tied into a closed loop with a **double fisherman's knot** (the standard joining knot for prusik loops). Two prusik loops of different lengths are typical: a short one (~12 inches when looped) for the foot, a longer one (~20 inches) for the waist or chest.
- The cord must be **thinner** than the rope it grips. A 6mm cord grips an 8–11mm rope well. A 7mm cord on a 9–10mm rope is the climbing-rescue standard.

## How to tie (three-wrap classic prusik)
1. Hold the closed cord loop. The knot (the double fisherman's) is somewhere on the loop — it will end up at the bottom of the finished prusik.
2. Place the loop behind the main rope.
3. Pass one end of the loop (the bight) through the other end, creating a girth hitch around the main rope.
4. You now have one wrap. The free end of the loop sticks down below the rope.
5. Repeat: pass the free end through the bight again, around the rope, making a **second** wrap.
6. Repeat once more for a **third** wrap.
7. Dress the wraps so they lie flat and parallel; pull the free end tight against the rope.
8. Clip a carabiner to the free end (the loop hanging below) for your foot or harness attachment.

A correctly dressed prusik with three wraps holds securely on dry, smooth rope. Use **four wraps** on icy, wet, or muddy rope. Use four wraps for heavier loads (rescue, hauling).

## How it works
When you weight the loop, the wraps cinch around the main rope and the friction holds. When you unweight and squeeze the wraps loose, the prusik slides. This is why prusiks must be sized correctly — a too-thick cord doesn't grip, a too-thin cord may break.

## Common uses
- **Ascending a rope** in self-rescue (foot prusik + waist prusik, alternating)
- **Self-belay** while rappelling (a prusik above your descender catches a fall)
- **Hauling** an injured climber or pulling tension on a guyline
- **Crevasse rescue** (a standard 3:1 Z-pulley uses prusiks as progress capture)
- **Tightening guylines and ridgelines** in camp

## Cautions
- Prusiks can be hard to release after heavy weighting; use the right cord diameter and dress carefully.
- Never use a prusik as a primary anchor — use it as a friction component within a larger system.
- Inspect the cord regularly for cuts, abrasion, and chemical damage. Replace at the first sign of wear.
- A prusik is a climbing technique. Learn from an instructor or guidebook before relying on it.

This is a foundational technical knot. Practice on the ground until you can tie it one-handed with cold fingers before you need it on a glacier.
