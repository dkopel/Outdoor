# Double Fisherman's Knot (Grapevine Bend)

## What it does
The double fisherman joins two ropes of equal diameter (or close) end-to-end with very high strength and a low chance of slipping. It's the standard knot for joining ropes for rappels, for creating Prusik loops, and for tying loop slings (cordelettes). Unlike a sheet bend or square knot, the double fisherman holds reliably even on slick modern climbing cord. The trade-off: it's hard to untie after heavy loading, so it's used when you want a permanent or semi-permanent joint.

## How to tie it
1. Lay the two rope ends side-by-side, pointing in opposite directions, overlapping by about 12 inches.
2. With the working end of the right rope, make two wraps around the left rope, working away from the left rope's tail.
3. Pass the working end back through the two wraps in the same direction the wraps were made. This creates the first half of the knot — looks like a double overhand around the other rope.
4. Repeat the mirror operation: working end of the left rope wraps twice around the right rope (working away from the right rope's tail), then back through both wraps.
5. Dress carefully — the wraps should lie flat, parallel, not crossed. Tighten by pulling both standing ends apart.
6. The two halves slide together when loaded, forming a tight compact knot.

## Use it for cordelettes and Prusik loops
A "cordelette" is a 18–25 foot length of 6–7mm accessory cord, with both ends joined by a double fisherman to make a closed loop. Versatile for climbing anchors, abseil backup, equalizing. A Prusik loop is a shorter length (24–36 inches) of cord, similarly joined into a loop, used as a friction hitch on a climbing rope. Both must be tied with a double fisherman, not a knot prone to slipping.

## Sizing the tails
Leave at least 3 inches of tail on each end after the knot is dressed and tightened. Climbing-rope joins for rappels often specify even longer tails (6+ inches) as backup against slip — though a well-tied double fisherman doesn't slip. Tails too short can pull through under load and unravel the knot. Tape can mark the recommended tail length; some climbers tape the ends after tying.

## Variations
The "triple fisherman" adds one more wrap on each side. Used on very slick high-modulus cord (Dyneema, Spectra) where doubles can slip. Most regular nylon and polyester ropes hold securely with the double; the triple is for special applications. The "flat overhand" (EDK / European Death Knot) is another rope-joining knot popular for rappels because it's flat to a wall and easy to untie — but it's controversial and beyond this app's scope.

## Untying after loading
After a fall or significant load, the double fisherman can be very difficult to untie. Strategies: bend the rope sharply at the knot to open the wraps, work the dressed strands loose one at a time, use a marlinspike or thin metal tool to start the unfurling. If you can't get it apart, cut the cord above the knot and tie a new loop — for safety gear, accept that cord may need replacement.
