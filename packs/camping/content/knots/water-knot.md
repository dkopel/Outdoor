# Water Knot

## What it does
The water knot (also called ring bend, tape knot) joins two ends of webbing (flat tubular or flat solid) to create a sling. It's the standard knot for tying anchor slings, equalizing webbing between fixed points, making personal slings from flat webbing, and any application where you need to join nylon or polyester webbing securely. Climbing's "tape knot" — universal in trad, anchoring, and rigging.

## How to tie it
1. Tie a loose overhand knot in one end of the webbing — single half-knot, leaving a long tail.
2. Take the other end of the webbing and trace back through the overhand knot, following the exact path of the first end in reverse. The two strands lie parallel through the knot.
3. Pull both ends to dress and tighten. Leave at least 3 inches of tail on each end — this is critical; water knots can slip if tails are too short.

The result is two parallel strands of webbing tied together with what looks like a doubled overhand knot.

## Sling sizing for climbing
A 24-inch sling (single-length) and 48-inch sling (double-length) are climbing standards. To tie one: cut webbing to length + 12 inches for the knot, tie a water knot, leave 3" tails, you've got your sling. Sewn slings (Bluewater, Mammut, BD) are stronger and more compact but harder to inspect and replace. Tied slings cost less, can be replaced cheaply when worn, but are slightly weaker (~10–15%) and bulkier.

## The slipping problem
Water knots have a documented failure mode: they slip slowly under cyclic loading, working themselves loose over time. Always leave 3"+ tails. Inspect slings before each climb — water knots with tails shorter than 2" should be retied or retired. Some climbers tape the tails or stitch them in place; both work. Slings that have been on routes left in place for years (anchor stations, rappel slings) often have water knots that have crept and need replacement.

## Don't use a water knot on rope
The water knot is specifically for flat webbing. On round rope, it tends to slip and is far less secure than a double fisherman's (for joining rope) or other rope-specific knots. The flat profile of webbing is what allows the water knot to work — it lays flat in the knot and is held by friction between the parallel strands.

## Inspecting a water knot
Look for: 3"+ tails on both sides, parallel strands lying flat through the knot (no twists), no obvious wear or fuzzing in the knot region, no UV damage (fading, brittleness). Webbing damages over time from UV light, abrasion, and chemical exposure (sweat, sunscreen, salt). Replace slings every 5 years even if they look fine, sooner if they see hard use.

## Alternative for joining webbing
The Beer Knot (for tubular webbing specifically) is stronger and more compact than the water knot, but it requires a stiff wire to feed one end inside the tubular sleeve of the other. Used for permanent gear-loop slings and anchor slings. Water knot remains the field-practical choice for most climbing applications.
