# Munter Hitch (Italian Hitch)

## What it does
The Munter hitch is a friction knot that provides belay and rappel functionality using only a carabiner — no belay device required. Essential as a backup when a climber drops or breaks their belay device, when rescue improvisation requires lowering someone, or in any situation where a device isn't available. UIAA-certified for climbing belay; widely taught as a fundamental knot in climbing and SAR (search and rescue).

## How to tie it
1. Hold the rope's standing end (going to the climber) on one side, working end on the other.
2. Form a loop in the rope by passing the standing end across, then twist the loop so it forms an "S" or "Z" shape.
3. Clip a locking carabiner through both legs of the loop, with the gate oriented to receive the rope.
4. The carabiner is now your friction point — pull the working end to add friction (lock off) or feed it through to give slack.

## How to belay with it
The Munter applies friction through the rope wrapping around the carabiner. Brake hand on the working (load) side; rope flips through the carabiner under control. To stop a fall: pull the brake hand back away from the carabiner. To give slack: feed rope through with both hands. The hitch flips orientation as load shifts, which is normal — practice this on a low-stakes belay before relying on it.

## Strengths
- Works with any locking carabiner (pear-shaped is best for smooth feeding).
- No belay device to lose, drop, or break.
- Universally available — every climber carries lockers.
- Provides strong friction — can hold any climber.
- Doubles as a rappel device.

## Limitations
- Twists the rope significantly during belay — old kinks and twists build up over a long pitch.
- Less smooth than a dedicated belay device (BD ATC, Petzl GriGri).
- Single-strand rappel only; not as smooth as devices for double-rope rappels.
- Requires the brake hand to be in the correct position — same as any belay technique, but the orientation is less obvious to new climbers.

## When to use it
- Belay device dropped or broken mid-route.
- Lowering an injured climber when a different rope setup is needed.
- Improvised rappel descent without a device.
- Self-rescue scenarios.
- As a primary belay choice when traveling ultralight (some alpinists do this).

## Practice before it matters
Tie and use the Munter on a low-angle slab or close to the ground, repeatedly, before you need it on a real route. The flipping action is unintuitive; muscle memory matters when adrenaline is involved. Pair the Munter with a "mule hitch" (lock-off) to free both hands while holding a load — essential for hauling and rescue.

## The Italian alternative name
In Europe, this knot is universally called the "Italian hitch" — same knot, same use. American climbers and Anglophone climbers call it the Munter (after Werner Munter, the Swiss mountain guide who popularized it). The terminology doesn't matter; the knot is the same.
