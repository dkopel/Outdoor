# Klemheist and Autoblock

## What friction hitches do
Friction hitches grip the climbing rope when loaded and slide easily when unloaded. They're used for: rappel backups (the "third hand"), ascending a fixed rope, escaping a belay system, hauling, and rescue improvisation. The Prusik is the classic friction hitch (covered separately); the Klemheist and Autoblock are alternatives with different strengths.

## Klemheist
**How to tie**: take a short loop of cord (or webbing — Klemheist works on webbing where Prusik struggles). Hold the loop along the climbing rope. Wrap the loop tightly around the climbing rope, going UP — 4–6 wraps. Pass the bottom of the loop up through the top loop. Pull down on the bottom loop to load.

**Properties**: directional — grips when loaded downward, less effective when loaded upward. Easier to slide than a Prusik when unloaded. Works on webbing as well as cord. Used as an ascending hitch on a fixed rope ("Klemheist + foot loop" climbing setup) and as a one-way friction grab.

## Autoblock (French Prusik)
**How to tie**: take a short loop of cord. Wrap the loop around the climbing rope 4–6 times — wraps lie next to each other, not on top of each other. Clip both ends of the loop into a single locking carabiner on your harness.

**Properties**: bidirectional — grips when loaded from either side. Easier to release under load than a Klemheist or Prusik. Standard "third hand" backup for rappels — clipped below the rappel device on the leg loop, it stops the rope automatically if you let go of the brake. Critical safety component for any rappel; some guides require it.

## When to use which
**Prusik**: ascending a fixed rope, hauling, rescue rigging. Holds heavy loads. Hard to slide when unloaded — used for static work.

**Klemheist**: ascending on webbing, single-direction friction grab, lighter loads than Prusik. Easier to slide when unloaded — used when frequent repositioning is needed.

**Autoblock**: rappel backup, situations needing bidirectional grip and easy release under load. Standard for "always" use on rappel.

## Cord sizing
Friction hitch cords should be ~60% the diameter of the climbing rope they're gripping. For a 9.8mm climbing rope, use 5–6mm accessory cord. Too thick and the hitch won't grip well; too thin and it can melt under load. Pre-tied loops (24"–36" of cord with a double fisherman) live on the harness as ready-to-use friction hitches.

## Rappel backup setup
1. Set up your rappel device.
2. Below the device, on your leg loop or belay loop, clip an autoblock loop around both rappel strands.
3. The autoblock should grip when slid up against the rappel device, slide down with the rope when you rappel.
4. Test by letting go of the brake hand briefly — autoblock should catch and stop you.
5. As you rappel, slide the autoblock down with your brake hand. If you lose control, it stops you automatically.

## Cautions
Friction hitches generate heat under load — a Prusik or Klemheist holding a fall on a thin rope can melt the cord. They're not designed to catch leader falls. Use them within their design purpose. Inspect cords regularly for glazing (shiny burnt-look on the surface), fuzzing, and damage. Replace any cord with visible heat damage.

## Climbing the rope on Klemheist + Prusik
Field improvisation for ascending a fixed rope: tie a Klemheist around the rope with a long loop of cord — stand in this. Tie a Prusik above the Klemheist, clip to your harness with a sling. To climb: lift the Klemheist (foot loop) by sliding it up; weight your harness loop (sit hanging from the Prusik). Then move the Prusik up. Alternating, you ascend. Slow but functional; learn the technique before you need it.
