# Altimeter Navigation

## How altimeters work
Barometric altimeters measure air pressure and convert to altitude. They're accurate within 50–100 feet when calibrated and stable; they drift as weather pressure changes. GPS altimeters use satellite triangulation; less precise but don't drift. Many outdoor watches and apps fuse both — barometric for short-term precision, GPS for periodic re-calibration. Knowing how your altimeter behaves is more useful than the absolute reading.

## Calibration
Calibrate at known elevation points: trailheads (signs often show elevation), summits, named features on the map, road crossings with known elevation. Re-calibrate any time you're at a known point — even if you calibrated at the trailhead in the morning, pressure may have changed by noon. Some altimeters auto-calibrate against GPS; check whether yours does and whether it's enabled.

## Using altitude to navigate
Altitude is a second axis of position. On a known trail with elevation profile, your altimeter tells you how far along you are without needing GPS. On bushwhacks, altitude tells you which contour line you're on; combine with bearing/handrail to find your X-Y position. Climbing a ridge, altitude tells you whether you've reached the saddle or summit ahead. In whiteout or dense forest, altitude is often the most reliable single piece of position data.

## Weather predictor
Pressure-based altimeters also work as barometers — they're the same sensor. An altimeter that reads progressively higher than your known elevation means barometric pressure is falling — weather is deteriorating. An altimeter reading progressively lower than known elevation means pressure is rising — weather is improving. Many outdoor watches have a barometric trend display; learn to read it. Sharp pressure drops over 3 hours often precede storms within 6–12 hours.

## Altitude vs elevation in maps
Maps show elevation above mean sea level (MSL) for North America, calibrated to the geoid. Most altimeters also display in MSL by default. Some maps use slightly different reference datums; double-check the map margin if elevations seem consistently off by hundreds of feet. GPS altitude is referenced to the WGS84 ellipsoid by default; high-quality units convert to MSL but cheaper ones may not.

## When altimeter matters most
Whiteout descent — you may need to navigate by altitude to a known saddle you can't see. Climbing in cloud — altitude tells you you've reached the summit before you can confirm visually. Off-trail with multiple parallel features — altitude tells you which ridge or drainage you're in. Multi-day weather watching — altimeter trends give you 6–12 hour forecast information independent of any weather service. Carry a known-good altimeter on any trip that involves significant elevation change.
