# Reading a Topographic Map

## What contour lines tell you
Contour lines connect points of equal elevation. The contour interval is in the legend — usually 20, 40, or 80 feet on USGS maps. Lines close together = steep terrain. Lines far apart = gentle. Lines that form V-shapes pointing uphill = a drainage or stream. V-shapes pointing downhill (ridges) point toward the valley. A closed circle is a hilltop or depression — if the inside has hash marks pointing inward, it's a depression. Reading contours fluently is the single most useful map skill in the backcountry.

## Scale and distance
A 1:24,000 USGS quad means 1 inch on the map = 24,000 inches on the ground = 2,000 feet, or about 0.4 miles. 1:25,000 European maps are nearly identical. 1:50,000 covers more ground but with less detail. Use the scale bar at the bottom of the map, not just the ratio — fold a strip of paper, mark your start and end points along your route, then lay it against the scale bar. Distance on the map is straight-line; actual hiking distance is longer because trails wind and climbs add time.

## Terrain features to recognize
Hill (a single rise with all contours forming closed loops), ridge (a long high feature with V-contours pointing down toward valleys on both sides), saddle (a low point on a ridge between two hills), valley (V-contours point uphill, a stream often runs through), draw (smaller version of a valley), spur (a smaller ridge running off a larger one), cliff (contour lines touching or crossing — sometimes marked with a hachure pattern), pass (a gap through a ridge, often where trails cross).

## Other map elements
Blue lines = water. Solid blue lines are perennial streams; dashed are intermittent — empty in dry season. Black solid lines are typically roads; black dashed are trails. Green tint indicates vegetation cover; white indicates rock, sand, or alpine. Black squares/rectangles are buildings or structures. Brown contours are land elevation. Purple or red overprints on USGS quads indicate features added in newer revisions. Always check the date on the bottom of the map — features change.

## Practical use
Before a trip, study the map at home. Trace your route with a finger. Estimate climb (count contour intervals you'll cross on the up). Identify "handrails" you'll use to navigate — features parallel to your route like a stream or ridge — and "catching features" you can't miss past your destination — a road, river, or major ridge that tells you you've gone too far. Mark expected campsites and water sources. On the trail, every 30 minutes, ask: "where am I on the map?" and check.
