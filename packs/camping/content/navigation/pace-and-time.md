# Pace, Time, and Distance

## Naismith's Rule (and improvements)
Naismith's classic rule for hiking time: 1 hour per 3 miles (5 km) of horizontal distance, plus 1 hour per 2,000 feet (600 m) of elevation gain. For most fit hikers with a moderate pack, this is too fast; Tranter's correction adjusts for fitness level and load — slow your pace by 25–50% for unfit hikers, heavy packs, bad weather, or technical terrain. For a beginner with a 40-lb pack: expect 1.5–2 mph horizontal and 1,000 ft/hour climb, max. Plan turnaround times around the slowest member.

## Pace counting
Find your personal pace count: walk a measured 100 meters on flat ground at a normal carrying-pack speed and count steps on one foot (every time the left foot lands). Most adults are between 60–70 paces per 100 meters. Repeat several times to find your average. In thick brush, snow, or steep ground, your pace count goes up — calibrate on similar terrain when stakes matter.

## Using pace count
On a bearing through poor visibility (whiteout, fog, dense forest), pace-count to measure distance covered. Combined with a compass bearing, this gives you a position estimate. After every 100 meters of pace count, slide a bean from one pocket to the other, drop a stone, or click a ranger bead — don't trust your memory under stress. Pace counting is more accurate than GPS over short distances in marginal conditions.

## Time discipline
Set a turnaround time for any out-and-back day trip. "We turn around at 1 PM regardless of where we are." A time-based limit beats a feeling-based one; you'll always feel like you can squeeze a little more. Reaching the summit at the cost of descending in the dark is a common pattern in mountain accidents. Build descent time into the plan — descents are often 60–80% of ascent time, not half.

## Reality checks
Match your time estimate against the day's pace every break. If you're behind schedule, either find time to make up (cut a side trip, push the pace) or shrink the day's plan (turn around early, camp at a closer spot). Don't double down on a plan that the morning is telling you won't work. Bad weather, illness, gear failure, and slower-than-planned ground all show themselves in the first two hours — listen to them.
