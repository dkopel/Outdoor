# GPS Basics

## What GPS does and doesn't do
A GPS receiver listens for signals from satellites and triangulates your position to within about 5–15 feet in clear sky. It tells you exactly where you are; it does not tell you where to go or what's safe ahead. Treat GPS as a precision tool that confirms map-and-compass work — not a replacement. A GPS with a dead battery is just a paperweight, and at high latitudes or in deep canyons, satellite reception can fail entirely.

## Waypoints and tracks
A waypoint is a saved location — your car, a water source, the trailhead, the spot you got cell signal. A track is a breadcrumb trail of where you've been. Both are gold for navigation. Before a trip: load waypoints for the trailhead, key trail junctions, water sources, and your planned campsites. During: drop a waypoint at any point you'd want to return to, especially before any side trip away from your pack. Save your track so you can backtrack exactly if needed.

## Datum and coordinates
USGS maps use NAD27 or WGS84 datums. Your GPS defaults to WGS84. Position read in the wrong datum can be off by hundreds of feet — enough to put you on the wrong side of a ridge in the dark. Set GPS to match the map. Coordinates are typically in lat/long (degrees minutes seconds, or decimal degrees) or UTM (a metric grid). UTM is faster to plot on a topo and gives precise grid references; lat/long is more universal.

## Battery management
GPS is greedy. A continuously-tracking GPS in cold weather can burn through a set of AA batteries in 6–8 hours. Phone GPS is even worse if the screen stays on. Practical disciplines: keep the device off most of the time, only turn on to check; carry 2x the batteries you think you need; use lithium AA batteries in cold weather; turn off backlights, Bluetooth, and Wi-Fi; keep electronics in an inner pocket near body heat in cold. Always have map and compass as the backup.

## Phone-as-GPS realities
Phones with offline maps (Gaia, AllTrails, OnX, Caltopo, FATMap, etc.) are excellent navigation tools. Download maps before you lose signal. Phone GPS works in airplane mode if the maps are cached — turning radios off saves battery. A waterproof case and a separate battery pack are practically required. Drop your phone in cold rain or break the screen and you're back to map and compass — practice both regularly.
