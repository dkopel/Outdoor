# Handrails, Catching Features, and Aim-Off

## Handrails
A handrail is a linear feature you follow that runs roughly parallel to your route — a stream, a ridge, a power line, a fence, a trail, the edge of a forest. Handrails are forgiving: as long as you stay along them, you can't get lost laterally. When planning a route, look for handrails first. If your destination is near a creek that flows from the mountain you're descending, follow the creek even if a direct line would be shorter — the creek won't move.

## Catching features
A catching feature is something past your destination that you cannot miss — a road, a major river, a cliff band, a long ridgeline. Use it as a backstop. If you've gone past your destination, the catching feature tells you so before you've gone wildly off. Example: aiming for a small saddle in dense forest, with a road one mile beyond. If you hit the road, you've overshot — back up and search carefully.

## Aim-off
When your destination is on a long handrail (a junction on a road, a campsite along a river), don't aim straight at it. Aim deliberately off to one side, then turn toward your destination when you hit the handrail. Otherwise you'll arrive at the handrail, not know whether the destination is left or right, and waste time guessing. Example: aiming for a trail junction on a stream — aim 100 meters upstream of the junction, hit the stream, follow it downstream until you find the junction.

## Boxing obstacles
To navigate around a lake, cliff, or impassable bog while keeping your bearing: turn 90° at the obstacle, count paces along the new direction until past the obstacle, turn 90° to resume original direction, walk past the obstacle, turn 90° back, and walk the same number of paces. You've boxed it and are back on the original line. Practice this in the woods before you need it.

## Mental discipline
Real-world navigation is mostly a series of small decisions, not heroic feats. Stop every 20–30 minutes and locate yourself on the map. Talk yourself through what you should see ahead before you see it ("we should drop into a draw with a stream in about 200 meters"). When something doesn't match — terrain doesn't look right, compass and ground disagree — stop immediately. Don't walk through doubt; correct it before it compounds.
