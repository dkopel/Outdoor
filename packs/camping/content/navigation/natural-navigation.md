# Natural Navigation (No Compass)

## Sun direction
In the Northern Hemisphere, the sun rises roughly in the east, is due south at solar noon, and sets in the west. In the Southern Hemisphere, the sun is due north at solar noon. "Solar noon" is when the sun is at its highest point — not necessarily noon on your watch. The path is more accurate in spring/fall than at solstices, and accurate only at low to mid latitudes. A vertical stick's shadow points away from the sun — at solar noon in the Northern Hemisphere, the shadow points due north.

## Watch method (rough, daylight)
With an analog watch set to local time: in the Northern Hemisphere, point the hour hand at the sun. The line that bisects the angle between the hour hand and 12 o'clock points roughly south. In the Southern Hemisphere, point 12 at the sun and bisect to the hour hand to find north. Inaccurate by up to 20° depending on season, time of day, and latitude — useful as a sanity check, not a primary method.

## Stars (Northern Hemisphere): Polaris
The North Star (Polaris) sits within about 1° of true north, so finding it tells you north precisely. Find the Big Dipper. The two stars at the front of the cup ("pointers") align in a line that points to Polaris, at roughly 5x the distance between them. Polaris is the bright-ish star at the end of the Little Dipper's handle. Polaris's altitude above the horizon equals your latitude — sometimes useful, more often just trivia.

## Stars (Southern Hemisphere): Southern Cross
There is no equivalent star directly over the South Pole. Find the Southern Cross constellation (a kite-shaped group, distinct from the larger "False Cross" nearby). Extend the long axis of the cross approximately 4.5 times its length down toward the horizon — the point you arrive at marks south on the horizon. Two "pointer stars" off to one side of the cross help confirm you've identified it correctly.

## Sanity-check natural navigation
Moss does not reliably grow on the north side of trees — it grows where moisture is, which varies by terrain, prevailing wind, and tree species. Ant hills, snow patterns, branch density, and similar folk methods are weak signals at best. Use sun and stars first; treat natural navigation as a coarse cross-check on a GPS or compass, not a primary method when stakes are high.
