# Map and Compass Basics

## Declination
A compass needle points to magnetic north; maps are drawn to true north (or grid north, in some military maps). The angle between them is "declination," varies by location, and changes slowly over years. Your topo map's margin states the declination at the time the map was made — adjust for current value from a public source before a trip. Some compasses have a declination adjustment built in (rotate a tiny screw); otherwise, you mentally add or subtract the declination every time you take or follow a bearing. Forgetting declination is the most common navigation mistake.

## Taking a bearing from the map
1. Lay the long edge of the compass baseplate along the line from where you are to where you want to go on the map.
2. Rotate the compass housing until the orienting lines inside it line up with the map's north–south grid lines (north arrow on the housing pointing to map north).
3. Read the bearing at the index line.
4. Add/subtract declination to convert to a field (magnetic) bearing.
5. Hold the compass level in front of you, rotate your body until the magnetic needle aligns with the orienting arrow, and walk the direction of travel arrow.

## Following a bearing
Pick a distinctive feature on your bearing — a tree, rock, or ridge bump — walk to it without watching the compass, then re-check the bearing from there and pick the next feature. This is much faster and more accurate than staring at the compass while you walk, which causes drift. In featureless country (whiteout, deep forest, prairie), follow the compass directly and resight every 20–50 steps.

## Triangulation (finding your position)
If you can see two or more identifiable landmarks on the map, take a magnetic bearing to each, convert to map (true) bearing, and draw a line from each landmark back along that bearing. You are somewhere along each line; where they cross is your position. Three landmarks give a small triangle ("cocked hat") whose center is your likely position. Triangulation requires identifying the landmarks correctly on both ground and map — a mistake there produces confident wrong answers.
