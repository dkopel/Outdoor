# When Your Boots Fail

## Sole separation
The most common boot failure on long trips: the sole detaches from the upper, usually starting at the toe. Field fix: clean both surfaces with a rag, dry as completely as possible, apply cyanoacrylate (super glue) or polyurethane glue along the separation, press together firmly, and weight it with a heavy pack or rock for 20+ minutes. If you don't have glue, wrap the entire toe area tightly with duct tape — multiple wraps, smooth out wrinkles. Cord lashed around the boot in a series of overlapping wraps works in a pinch. The fix is temporary; finish the trip and replace.

## Blown seam at the heel or ankle
A torn seam in the boot upper can be field-stitched. You need a heavy-duty needle (sail or upholstery needle) and dental floss or stout cord. Stitch using a saddle stitch or baseball stitch — two needles, alternating sides, pulling tight after each pass. Reinforce the stitching with duct tape on the inside and outside of the seam. The repair is rarely waterproof; reapply seam grip or shoe goo at home before next trip. Heavy stitching may need a thimble; carry one with the repair kit.

## Broken laces
Replace with paracord, parachute cord, or even bootlace cordage from elsewhere on your gear. Tie a figure-eight stopper knot in the new lace end so it doesn't pull through the eyelet. If the new lace is too thick for eyelets, skip every other eyelet — boots can be functional with reduced lacing. Always carry one spare lace; they're cheap and weigh nothing. A spare pair of laces also stops a single broken lace from ending a trip.

## Hot spots and blisters mid-trip
Treat at first sensation, before a blister forms. Stop, check the spot, apply tape or moleskin smoothly so wrinkles can't form under it. Rebreak the boot in by tightening laces appropriately — too loose around the heel causes friction, too tight on the instep causes pressure blisters. Change socks at midday if they're wet (sweat or stream crossing). Carry spare socks. For deep blisters, see first-aid.blisters and first-aid.wound-care.

## Wet boots
Wet boots are unavoidable on long trips. Avoid them sleeping with you in the bag — they don't dry, and they make your bag wet. Stuff with crumpled paper or dry rags overnight to wick moisture; remove and re-stuff each morning. If you're near a low fire, prop them near (not in) the heat — boots burn beyond rescue in seconds if too close. Wet leather dries best slowly; aggressive heat cracks and shrinks leather. Leather conditioner at home extends boot life.

## Replace before you have to
Boots wear out gradually. Loss of grip on familiar terrain, sole tread worn smooth, persistent hot spots that didn't happen before, separating soles, blown seams that you've already patched — these are signs to retire the boot, not push another trip. Buy boots before you need them, break them in (5–10 short walks on varied terrain) before any committing trip. Field-repaired boots are still field-repaired; they fail more often than new ones.
