# Sleeping Pad and Water Filter Repairs

## Find a pad leak
Inflate the pad fully. Listen — small leaks sometimes whistle in quiet conditions. If you can't hear it, immerse sections of the pad in water (a lake, a wide stream, or a basin) and watch for bubbles. No water? Smear the surface with soapy water and look for bubbles rising. Once you find the leak, dry the spot completely before patching — wet patches don't bond. Mark the leak with a piece of tape before deflating.

## Patch the leak
Most pad manufacturers include a few patches; commercial Tenacious Tape works on most fabrics. Round off patch corners (sharp corners peel up). Clean the area with alcohol if you have it. Press the patch firmly for 30+ seconds and let cure for an hour before reinflating. For larger holes or fabric tears, you may need two layers — one inside (through the valve, with a stick to position) and one outside, like a sandwich. Cyanoacrylate seam sealers (Aquaseal) work on stubborn leaks but need 24+ hours to fully cure.

## Pad won't hold air at the valve
Valve seals fail. Check the o-ring or gasket — debris, hair, or pine sap can be lodged in there. Clean carefully with a damp cloth. Replace the o-ring if you carry spares (some warranties include them). If the valve mechanism is damaged, your pad may need warranty replacement — emergency field option is to cover the valve with duct tape after every inflation, but it's slow.

## Water filter clogging
Clogged filters produce dramatically lower flow rates. Strategies vary by filter type. Squeeze filters (Sawyer, Katadyn BeFree): backflush with the included syringe, or invert and shake vigorously. Pump filters (MSR, Katadyn): disassemble per the manual and scrub or replace the filter element. Gravity filters: similar backflushing. Never poke anything into the filter media — you'll create a channel that lets pathogens through. Filters have a useful life measured in liters or years; replace when filtering 50%+ slower than new.

## Filter freezing
A wet filter cartridge that freezes is ruined — ice crystals open the membrane to micron-larger pores, letting pathogens through. There's no way to test for this damage in the field; if a filter froze, replace it. Strategies to prevent: keep wet filters inside your sleeping bag at night in cold weather, drain the filter completely before storing if temperatures will drop, carry chemical treatment as backup. Boiling water always works as a backup.

## Backup treatment
Never rely on a single water treatment method on a multi-day trip. Carry chemical treatment (chlorine dioxide tablets are compact, work against everything, and last for years on the shelf) as backup to your primary filter. Boiling works if you have stove and fuel. A combination — filter for clarity and protozoa, plus chemical for viruses or filter failure — is the highest-confidence field setup. Test your full water treatment system at home before the trip; some filters need flushing before first use.
