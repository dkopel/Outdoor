# Dressing in Layers

Most cold-weather injuries are not from extreme cold — they're from being wet, sweaty, and unprepared in moderately cold conditions. The classic three-layer system manages moisture and adjustable insulation so you stay dry and the right temperature across changing activity and weather.

## Base layer — manage moisture
Worn next to skin. Its job is to move sweat off your body so you don't get chilled when you stop moving. Use synthetic (polyester, polypropylene) or merino wool. **Avoid cotton** in cold weather — cotton absorbs sweat and loses all insulation when wet ("cotton kills"). A long-sleeve top and long-john bottom are the standard for cold trips.

## Mid layer — insulate
Worn over the base layer. Traps warm air. Use fleece, down, or synthetic puffy. Down is lighter and more compressible but useless when wet; synthetic insulation retains some warmth wet and dries faster. In wet climates, prefer synthetic.

## Outer layer (shell) — block wind and water
Worn over the insulation. A windproof, water-resistant or waterproof shell. In dry cold (interior winter mountains), a softshell is breathable and adequate. In wet cold (coastal, shoulder seasons, rain), use a hardshell rain jacket with sealed seams. The shell goes on when wind picks up or precipitation starts; comes off when you're working hard and need to dump heat.

## Adjusting on the move
Hike at a pace that doesn't make you sweat heavily. If you start sweating, stop and remove a layer **before** you soak the base. When you stop for a break, immediately add a layer — heat loss in still air after exertion is rapid. The mantra: "Be bold, start cold" — start a climb slightly underdressed knowing you'll warm up in 5–10 minutes.

## Extremities
Up to 30% of body heat loss comes from the head and neck. A wool or synthetic beanie and a buff or scarf are inexpensive and very effective. Gloves with a thin liner under a heavier insulated glove or mitten allow dexterity for tasks (camp chores, maps) without exposing skin. Wool or synthetic socks; bring spares dry packed.

## Cotton is dangerous
The single biggest layering mistake is wearing cotton (jeans, t-shirts, sweatshirts) into cold or wet conditions. Cotton offers no insulation when wet, dries slowly, and is the leading clothing factor in wilderness hypothermia cases. Save cotton for warm-weather day trips.
