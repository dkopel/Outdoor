# Microclimates and Local Weather

## Why a 10-foot move changes the temperature
A campsite at the bottom of a valley can be 15–20°F colder than a bench 50 feet above it on a calm clear night — cold dense air sinks and pools. South-facing slopes get more sun and stay warmer in winter; north-facing slopes hold snow longer and stay cool in summer. Ridgetops are windier (often by 2–3x). The choice of where to stop, camp, or pitch a tarp matters more than gear in many situations.

## Cold pockets to avoid
Valley bottoms in mountain country on clear calm nights. Hollows and depressions where cold air collects. Open meadows surrounded by forest (no canopy to slow radiative cooling). Just downhill of glaciers or large snowfields. Frost forms in these spots on otherwise mild nights. The bench 30 feet above the valley floor is often dramatically warmer; the leeward side of a tree, log, or boulder breaks wind without trapping cold.

## Warm spots to find
South-facing slopes during cool weather. Sheltered alcoves below cliffs that face the sun. Inside dense conifer canopy in cold weather — branches catch radiating cold and the trapped air below stays warmer. Inside a tarp/tent shelter in any season (anywhere from 5–20°F warmer than outside depending on conditions). Near (not on) large rocks that hold daytime heat.

## Aspect and seasonal travel
North aspect = colder, wetter, shadier; holds snow into early summer in mountains. South aspect = warmer, drier, often sees less rain shadowing. East aspect gets morning sun but cools fast in afternoon. West aspect gets sun late, often warmest in early evening. For early-season hiking, prefer south-facing trails to clear snow first; for hot summer hikes, prefer north-facing or canyon-shaded routes.

## Wind shadow
Trees, large rocks, ridgelines, and boulder fields all create wind shadow on their downwind side. Cooking, sleeping, and changing layers are dramatically easier in wind shadow. Even a small bush or boulder helps. Be aware that "wind shadow" assumes a steady wind direction; if wind shifts during the night, your protected spot can become exposed and vice versa. In storm conditions, expect wind to shift; pitch tents accordingly.

## Putting it together
Before stopping for the night, scout 100 yards in each direction. Look for the bench that's above the cold sink but below the windy ridge. Find tree cover that breaks the wind without dropping cold dew all night. Reconsider obvious flat open spots in valleys — they're easy to walk to but often coldest. In summer, water-source-adjacent campsites are common but also where bugs are worst; the bench 200 feet up may be far better. Local knowledge from rangers, guidebooks, or experienced trip partners pays off.
