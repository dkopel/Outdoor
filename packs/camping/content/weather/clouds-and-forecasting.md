# Reading Clouds in the Field

## High clouds (above 18,000 ft)
Cirrus — wispy "horsetails" — generally signal fair weather but if they're thickening, expect a warm front and rain within 24–36 hours. Cirrocumulus — small white patches in a rippled "mackerel sky" — suggests cool, fair weather but unstable air aloft. Cirrostratus — thin uniform sheet causing a halo around the sun or moon — is a strong predictor of rain or snow within 12–24 hours, especially after low clouds also appear.

## Mid clouds (6,500–18,000 ft)
Altocumulus — gray-white patches in waves or rolls — often appear on warm humid mornings before afternoon thunderstorms. Altostratus — uniform gray sheet that dims the sun without producing a halo — usually brings steady rain or snow within hours. Lenticular (lens-shaped) clouds parked over mountain peaks indicate very strong winds aloft and often herald an approaching storm; mountaineers treat them as warnings to delay exposed routes.

## Low clouds (under 6,500 ft) and storm signs
Stratus — uniform low gray ceiling — produces drizzle, often persistent. Stratocumulus — lumpy gray ceiling with breaks — usually means dry but overcast. Cumulus humilis — small puffy fair-weather clouds — fine on their own. The threat starts when cumulus build vertically into towering cumulus or cumulonimbus: dark bases, anvil tops, visible internal motion. These produce thunderstorms, often within 1–3 hours of first appearance.

## Pressure and wind cues
Falling barometric pressure (on an altimeter that's drifting upward when you're staying at the same elevation) signals deteriorating weather. Rising pressure (altimeter reads lower for the same actual elevation) signals improving weather. Wind shifting direction, especially from a previously calm or steady direction, often marks a front passage. Sudden temperature drops of 10°F+ in summer indicate a cold front and typically thunderstorms. Calm before storms is real — windless air ahead of a thunderstorm cell is common.

## Mountain-specific patterns
Summer alpine: morning cumulus build to afternoon thunderstorms most days from late June through August. Plan to summit early (off by noon, off ridges by 11 in active periods). Valley fog clearing slowly through morning is normal; persistent valley fog suggests inversion and worsening weather. Lenticular over high peaks = strong winds aloft, delay exposed climbing. Snow at the start of a winter pattern often dumps multiple feet over 2–3 days; storm cycles run on a 3–7 day rhythm. Listen to local forecasts daily.
