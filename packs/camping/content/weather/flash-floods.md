# Flash Floods

## Why they kill so many people
Flash floods are the deadliest weather hazard in many parks. Six inches of moving water can knock an adult off their feet; two feet floats most vehicles. The water can be miles from where it rains — a clear sky overhead in a slot canyon does not mean you're safe when a thunderstorm is dumping 20 miles upstream. Most flash flood deaths happen to people who drove or walked into water that "didn't look that bad."

## Where the risk is highest
Slot canyons in the Southwest (Zion, Escalante, Antelope), arroyos and dry washes anywhere in the desert, canyon-bottom trails (Grand Canyon side canyons), low-water road crossings, and any narrow drainage downstream of mountains during summer monsoon season. Late-season storms over the Sierra, Rockies, and Cascades can also produce flash flooding in tight valleys. Check the NWS forecast for flood watches/warnings for your specific area before any canyon trip.

## Warning signs
Distant thunder you can hear (lightning is within 6 miles). Building thunderheads upstream — not over you. A rising "freight train" sound that's not wind. Sudden change in stream color (brown, muddy where it was clear). Floating debris like sticks moving fast in what was a clear shallow stream. The smell of wet earth or sage when there's no rain where you are. Any of these in canyon country: get out of the canyon NOW. You have minutes, not hours.

## Action if caught
Get to high ground immediately — not high ground 100 yards away if there's a 20-foot bench right above you. Drop your pack if it slows you. Climb whatever you can climb — flash flood water rises fast and falls fast; even 10 feet of elevation often saves people. Once high, stay there until the water drops below pre-flood levels for at least 30 minutes; secondary surges happen. Cell signal often fails in slot country; don't count on calling for help.

## Prevention is most of the answer
Get the NOAA forecast for the specific canyon district, not just the city nearest. Ask rangers — they know the local weather pattern and slot canyon timing. Cancel slot trips when thunderstorms are forecast within a wide radius upstream, not just overhead. Camp on high ground above streams, never on sand bars or in tight gullies. Park vehicles on high ground; flash floods drown vehicles overnight regularly. "Turn around, don't drown" applies to feet too.
