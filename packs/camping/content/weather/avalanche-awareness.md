# Avalanche Awareness

## This is an awareness primer, not training
Backcountry winter travel in avalanche terrain requires formal training (an AIARE Level 1 course or equivalent, hands-on, with snow practice). This chunk gives you enough awareness to recognize when you're in avalanche country and need to either get the training or stay out. If you've never had avalanche training and you're looking at snow-covered slopes between 25° and 50° steep — turn around. The data does not save you here; the training does.

## Terrain that slides
The danger zone is slopes between 25° and 50° steep, with most slides occurring on 35–45°. Below 25° rarely slides. Above 50° usually sluffs continuously without building up. Convex rolls, leeward (downwind) bowls where snow loads, terrain traps below slopes (gullies, ravines, cliffs, trees) that turn a small slide into a fatal one. Aspect matters: north-facing slopes hold instability longer; sun-warmed south slopes can become wet avalanche terrain in spring.

## Conditions that load the snowpack
New snow loading more than 1 inch per hour. Wind transport (slopes loaded leeward of a ridge). Rapid temperature rise. Rain on snow. Recent avalanche activity in the area — if you can see fresh slides, more are coming. Cracks shooting out from your skis or boots ("whumpfing" sounds underfoot) is a clear signal the snowpack is collapsing. If you're seeing or hearing these, get off the slope and onto safer terrain.

## Travel discipline (if you're trained)
Cross suspect slopes one at a time, with the others watching from safe terrain. Stay clear of terrain traps. Carry beacon, probe, and shovel — and know how to use all three to find a buried partner in under 7 minutes. Stay back from cornices on ridgelines (cornices break further back than you'd think). Time of day matters: cold morning = more stable, sun-warmed afternoon = less stable. Check the local avalanche center forecast every morning of your trip.

## If you're caught
Yell so others know your last seen point. Try to ski/board off the slab toward the side. As snow hits, fight to stay near the surface — swimming motions help. As snow stops, get a hand toward the surface and create an air space in front of your face with the other hand. Try to stay calm; rescue happens fast or not at all. If you survive, even partly buried, do not move until certain the slope is stable — secondary slides happen.

## The real rule
For untrained outdoor people: avoid winter backcountry travel in mountainous terrain unless you're with someone trained, in good visibility, and conservative about terrain choice. Snowshoe or ski on flat valley trails, not slopes. Don't ascend or descend snow-covered slopes in the danger range. Avalanche country kills experienced people every winter; the only consistently safe play for the untrained is to stay out.
