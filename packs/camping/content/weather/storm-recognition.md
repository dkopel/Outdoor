# Recognizing Approaching Weather

## Clouds tell most of the story
Cumulus clouds (puffy, fair-weather) building vertically into cumulonimbus (anvil-topped towers) signals thunderstorms within hours — head down off ridges before the towers fully develop. Lenticular clouds (lens-shaped, often parked over peaks) mean very strong winds aloft and often signal an approaching front. A "mackerel sky" of high cirrus or cirrocumulus often precedes a warm front by 24–48 hours, bringing steady rain. Halos around the sun or moon (caused by high cirrus) often precede precipitation within a day.

## Wind, temperature, and pressure
Rapidly dropping temperature in summer, especially with a wind shift, is a cold front and often arrives with thunderstorms and a sudden rain band. Wind picking up from a previously calm direction may indicate an approaching front. Falling barometric pressure (which you can feel as a headache or notice in altimeters reading higher than usual) means worsening weather; rising pressure means improving. Watches without altimeter functions, plus the simple "barometric tendency over 3 hours" reading on most outdoor watches, are reliable warning signs.

## Mountain-specific patterns
Afternoon thunderstorms in summer alpine country are a near-daily pattern: warm humid morning air rises, builds cumulus by noon, develops into cumulonimbus by 2–4 PM, and breaks down by evening. Plan to summit early, be off ridges by 11 AM–noon in active thunderstorm season. Valley fog clearing mid-morning is normal; valley fog refusing to clear suggests an inversion and likely deteriorating weather. Lenticulars over the highest peaks are a near-certain sign to delay an exposed climb.

## Time to act
A general rule: act 30 minutes earlier than feels necessary. Backcountry weather changes faster than you expect, especially in summer alpine and on coasts. The cost of descending early and being wrong is minor (a few extra miles, an early lunch). The cost of being right and stranded high during a thunderstorm or whiteout is severe. When weather is questionable, the simpler call is almost always the safer one — turn around, descend, take shelter early.
