# Lightning Safety

## When you're at risk
"If you can hear thunder, lightning is close enough to strike you." That single rule covers most decisions. The "30-30 rule" formalizes it: when the time between a flash and its thunder is 30 seconds or less, the storm is within 6 miles (10 km) and dangerous; do not go outside until 30 minutes after the last thunder. Storms move fast in mountains — what looks far can be on you in 15 minutes. Watch the western or upwind sky in afternoon thunderstorm country, and start descending before you hear thunder, not after.

## The best shelter
A fully enclosed building or a hard-topped vehicle (windows up). The metal frame of a car conducts current around the occupants — pickup trucks with cabs work; open Jeeps and convertibles do not. In the backcountry where no shelter exists, get below ridgelines and treelines if possible. Descend off summits and ridges immediately when you hear thunder; even before. Time the descent so you're below treeline and off exposed rock before the storm arrives.

## If caught in the open
If you cannot reach shelter and a storm is overhead, assume the lightning position: crouch low on the balls of your feet, heels touching, head down, hands over ears. Stay on a closed-cell foam pad if you have one (insulates from ground currents). Do not lie flat — ground current spreading from a strike can be fatal at 100+ feet. Spread out from companions (10+ meters apart) so a single strike doesn't injure the whole party. Stay away from tall isolated trees, water, fences, and metal.

## After a strike
A lightning strike victim is safe to touch — they don't carry residual charge. Begin CPR immediately if there's no pulse or no breathing; lightning often stops heart and breathing without other damage, and victims can be revived. Treat burns conservatively. Check for ear-drum rupture, eye damage, and confusion. Evacuate every strike victim to medical care — internal injuries (cardiac, neurological) can appear hours later.
