# Wind Chill

Wind chill is the temperature your skin "feels" from the combined effect of cold air and wind. Wind strips heat from exposed skin much faster than still air does, which is why a 25°F day at 30 mph wind can cause frostbite to bare skin in 30 minutes, while the same 25°F with no wind would not.

## How to read a wind chill chart
The NWS wind chill chart shows actual temperature (top row) and wind speed (left column); the cell at the intersection is the wind chill. Frostbite times are also shaded on the chart:
- Frostbite possible in 30 minutes: wind chill around -19°F
- Frostbite possible in 10 minutes: wind chill around -32°F
- Frostbite possible in 5 minutes: wind chill around -48°F

These are times for **exposed, dry skin** — bare cheeks, nose, fingers without gloves. Wet skin freezes faster.

## What wind chill does not change
Wind chill only affects living tissue. It does not change the freezing point of water in your bottle, the rate at which an engine cools, or the temperature recorded by a thermometer. A still-water source at 35°F real temperature will not freeze due to "wind chill alone."

## In the backcountry
- Wind exposure is highest on ridges, exposed passes, and open snowfields. A wind-protected campsite a few hundred feet off a ridge is dramatically warmer.
- Travel into wind in the morning when you can; consider routing so you have a tailwind on tired returns.
- A windproof outer layer is more valuable than a heavier insulation layer in windy cold.
- Cover all skin: balaclava or buff, hood, mittens, gaiters.
- Check the forecast wind speed, not just temperature. Two days with the same low can be very different trips.

## Frostbite first aid (brief)
Bring affected tissue back to body temperature gradually using skin-to-skin contact (hands in armpits) or warm water (99–104°F if a thermometer is available, otherwise "comfortably warm" against the inside of an elbow). Do not rub the tissue, do not warm with direct heat (stove, fire), and do not thaw if there is any chance of refreezing — partial thaw followed by refreeze causes much worse damage than staying frozen until evacuation. Severe frostbite needs hospital care.

This is general guidance — refer to first-aid documentation for full frostbite and hypothermia treatment.
