# Acclimatization

## What's actually happening
At elevation, less oxygen per breath reaches your blood. The body responds over hours to days: faster breathing, faster heart rate, increased red blood cell production, kidney changes that raise blood pH. Full acclimatization to a given altitude takes 1–3 weeks. Partial acclimatization (enough to function but not yet optimal) happens over 2–4 days. Pushing higher before partial acclimatization sets in is the cause of most altitude illness.

## The standard rules
Above ~10,000 ft (3,000 m), don't increase your sleeping altitude by more than 1,000–1,500 ft (300–450 m) per day. Take a rest day every 3,000 ft (900 m) of net sleeping-altitude gain. Day-hiking higher than your camp ("climb high, sleep low") is encouraged — it triggers acclimatization without risking sleeping at unacclimatized altitude. Below 8,000 ft (2,400 m), altitude illness is rare; above 8,000 ft, it's common.

## Genetics is real
Some people acclimatize fast and have no symptoms even on aggressive itineraries. Others get AMS at 8,000 ft no matter how slowly they ascend. Fitness does not predict altitude tolerance — elite athletes often suffer at altitude because they push harder. Previous altitude experience predicts future tolerance somewhat, but not perfectly. If you got AMS last trip, plan for slow acclimatization this trip. Don't assume your buddy who climbed Kilimanjaro will breeze through your trip.

## Pre-trip strategies
You can't truly pre-acclimatize at sea level, but you can do things that help: hypoxic tent rentals simulate altitude during sleep at home for 2–4 weeks pre-trip (mixed evidence, but used by serious climbers). Climbing a lower altitude first and then going higher works as a stepped acclimatization. Some climbers use altitude medications under medical supervision. For most outdoor people, the most effective preparation is the schedule: build in extra days at intermediate altitudes.

## Things that worsen acclimatization
Alcohol and sleeping medications (depress breathing, reduce night-time oxygen). Dehydration. Cold (the body burns more oxygen). Hard exertion in the first 24 hours at altitude. Inadequate carbohydrate intake. Smoking. Some prescription medications including some antibiotics and sedatives. Ask a doctor pre-trip about any medications you take and altitude interactions.

## When acclimatization fails
Symptoms of AMS appearing within 24 hours of arriving at altitude mean you've ascended too fast for your body. Stop ascending. Rest. Hydrate. Eat. If symptoms don't resolve in 24–48 hours, descend even just 1,000 ft and try again. Symptoms worsening means descend immediately. Pushing through clear AMS leads to HACE or HAPE, which are life-threatening. There is no shame in coming down; the mountain isn't going anywhere.
