# Leave No Trace Principles

## Plan ahead and prepare
Knowing the regulations and special concerns of the area, preparing for extreme weather, and scheduling so you don't have to take shortcuts all reduce your impact. Map the area before you go, know your skill level honestly, and pre-package food to minimize trash in the field. A well-prepared group is also a safer group — Leave No Trace and trip safety overlap.

## Travel and camp on durable surfaces
On established trails, stay on them — cutting switchbacks accelerates erosion and creates new scars. In the high country off-trail, walk on rock, gravel, or snow when possible. Camp on durable surfaces: established sites, bare ground, or dry vegetation. Avoid fragile plants — alpine and desert ecosystems take decades to recover from a single boot path. Wet meadows and lakeshores are especially vulnerable; camp 200 ft (60 m) from water.

## Dispose of waste properly
Pack out all trash — including food scraps, which attract animals and don't always decompose. For human waste, dig a 6–8 inch deep cathole 200 ft from water, trails, and camp, do your business, and bury thoroughly. In some heavily used or alpine environments, pack out solid human waste in a wag bag. Toilet paper should be packed out — buried TP gets dug up by animals. Strain dishwater and pack out the food bits; scatter the gray water widely.

## Minimize campfire impacts
Use a stove instead of a fire where possible — they're lower-impact, faster to set up, and don't depend on dry wood. If you build a fire, use an existing ring, keep it small, burn only down wood from the ground (not standing dead), and burn all wood to white ash. Scatter cool ash and dismantle any new fire rings before leaving. In many high-impact areas, fires are restricted or prohibited entirely.

## Respect wildlife and other visitors
Observe from a distance with binoculars or a long lens. Never feed wildlife — it changes their behavior and makes them dependent or dangerous. Store food properly to keep animals wild. Yield to others on trail according to local convention (uphill hikers usually have right-of-way; everyone yields to horses). Keep noise down — wilderness is a sound experience as much as a visual one for many visitors.
