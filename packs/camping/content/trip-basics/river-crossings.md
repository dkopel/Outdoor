# River and Stream Crossings

## Why crossings are dangerous
Moving water is far stronger than its surface suggests. Water knee-deep at 5 mph exerts about 65 pounds of force on your shins; at thigh-deep it's enough to sweep most adults off their feet. Cold water (most mountain streams) shuts down muscle control within minutes, so even good swimmers can't self-rescue. Most backcountry river-crossing deaths happen when the crossing looked easier than it was. Treat every unbridged crossing as a real decision, not a routine.

## Scout the crossing
Walk 50–100 meters up and downstream looking for the best line. Best is usually: wide and shallow (flow spreads out and slows), gravel-bed (firmer footing than mud or moss-covered rocks), no log jams or strainers downstream (those drown people), and an obvious exit on the far side. Avoid crossing right above a drop, falls, or rapid — if you lose footing you'll be carried over it. Crossing earlier or later in the day matters: glacial streams run highest in late afternoon after snowmelt, lower at dawn.

## Decide whether to cross
Don't cross water above your thighs in significant current. Don't cross moving water that's brown, churning, or carrying debris — those signal a flood pulse, often from upstream rain. Don't cross if there's no clear exit on the far side, or if a rescue would be impossible. If conditions are wrong, your options are: wait (often hours fixes it, especially with morning low flows), find a different crossing point, find a fallen log bridge, or turn back. Backing up a day or two is not a failure; it's the right call.

## Crossing technique
Unbuckle your pack hip belt and sternum strap before stepping in. If you go down, you must be able to ditch the pack so it doesn't pin you underwater. Use trekking poles or a sturdy stick on the upstream side as a third leg. Cross at a slight downstream angle, never face the current. Move sideways with small shuffles, keep two contact points with the bottom at all times. For a group: face upstream in a tight line/triangle with arms locked, strongest person upstream as the wedge breaking the current.

## If you get knocked over
Don't try to stand in fast water — feet get pinned in rocks (foot entrapment is a leading drowning cause). Roll onto your back, feet pointed downstream, head up, arms out for balance. Use your feet to push off rocks. Look downstream and choose your line. Swim aggressively toward shore at a downstream angle when you see an opportunity. Get out of cold water as fast as possible and get into dry clothes — hypothermia after immersion can develop in 30 minutes.

## After the crossing
Change into dry socks. Wring out shorts. Move briskly for 5–10 minutes to rewarm. Inspect feet for cuts or bruises. Re-buckle the pack. Make notes about the conditions for the return trip — water that's crossable in the morning may be impassable in the afternoon. If a crossing was at the edge of safe and the return is days away, route-plan an alternate exit if possible.
