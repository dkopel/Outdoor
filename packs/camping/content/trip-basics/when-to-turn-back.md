# When to Turn Back

Most backcountry accidents are not single bad decisions — they are chains of small "we'll keep going" decisions that compound. The skill of recognizing when to turn back is the most important safety skill in the outdoors and the hardest to develop.

## Set a turnaround time before you leave
Decide before you start: "If I am not at the summit / lake / pass by [time], I turn around regardless of how close I am." A hard time-based limit is much easier to obey than a soft "I'll see how I feel" rule. It also removes the conversation from the moment when you are tired, summit-focused, and biased toward continuing.

## Watch for the warning signs
Turn around if you observe any of these, especially in combination:
- Weather worsening (rain, thunder, falling temperature, fog rolling in)
- A group member is becoming hypothermic, dehydrated, or visibly exhausted
- Visibility is dropping below 100 yards
- Route conditions are harder than expected (snow, river crossing, steep terrain, missing trail markers)
- You are off-route and not sure where you are
- Your turnaround time has passed
- Anyone in the group is uncomfortable continuing
- An injury has occurred, even a minor one
- You're rushing because you're behind schedule

Any one of these alone is a yellow flag. Two or more is red.

## The "vote no" rule
In a group, anyone has the right to turn the trip around without justification. No "convincing" required. Consensus to continue must be unanimous. This protects the quiet member who senses something is wrong but doesn't want to disappoint the group. Adopt it explicitly with your party at the trailhead.

## Summit fever is real
Investing time, money, and effort in a trip makes us irrationally reluctant to walk away. This is "sunk cost" thinking and it kills people. The mountain doesn't owe you a summit just because you trained for it. The right move is almost always: turn around, come back another time.

## The trip out is half the trip
Most fatal incidents happen on the descent, after the goal is achieved and people relax their attention while they are tired. Plan energy, daylight, and water to support a safe return — not just to reach the destination.

A trip you walk away from is a successful trip.
