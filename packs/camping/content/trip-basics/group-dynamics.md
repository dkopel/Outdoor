# Hiking and Camping in a Group

## Match pace to the slowest
The group pace is the pace of the slowest member. Pushing them harder is how you get injuries, bonks, and resentment. Either accept their pace, or split into faster/slower groups with clear meeting points. Strong hikers carrying lighter loads can take group gear (water, food) to relieve the slowest member. Never leave anyone alone in the backcountry without explicit consent and a plan for the next checkpoint.

## Communication and decision-making
Agree on the day's plan at breakfast: where you're going, expected time, key turn-arounds, weather plan. Whoever is "trip leader" doesn't dictate but is responsible for decisions when consensus fails. Real safety calls (turn around, evacuate, no summit today) belong to whoever is most cautious — the bar is "any one no = no," not "majority rules." Conflicts about pace, route, gear, or food choices are normal; address them at camp not on the trail.

## Common roles
Even informal groups benefit from rough role assignment: navigator (carries the map, checks position regularly), sweep (last person, ensures no one falls behind), medic (carries the first-aid kit, knows what's in it), water (knows the day's water plan, treats group water). Roles can rotate by day for variety. Larger groups benefit from a designated trip leader and assistant; small groups can flatten the structure.

## Cooking and camp chores
Distribute camp work. Common split: one person filters water, another lights stove, third sets up tents, fourth deals with food prep. Cleaning shouldn't fall on the cook. Make this explicit at camp — silent expectations breed resentment. Strong cooks may want to lead meal prep; that's fine if others do dishes. Trips where one or two people do all the work end badly.

## Conflict
Multi-day backcountry trips amplify small frustrations. Hunger, fatigue, cold, and lack of sleep make people snippier. Most "conflicts" resolve with a snack, water, and a 15-minute break. Real issues — about pace, route, gear competence, decision-making style — should be discussed at camp privately, not on the trail in front of everyone. Don't escalate at altitude or after a hard day; sleep on it. After-trip debriefs often reveal what went well and what should change next time.

## Safety culture
The strongest backcountry groups have a culture where any member can call a halt and be taken seriously. "I'm not comfortable with this crossing" stops the group, period — no convincing, no "come on you'll be fine." The same applies to weather windows, route choices, and pace. People who feel safe to raise concerns will raise the right ones; people who feel they'll be dismissed will hide trouble until it's too late.
