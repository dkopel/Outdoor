# If You Get Lost — S.T.O.P.

The moment you realize you don't know where you are, the worst thing you can do is keep walking in hopes of "finding the trail." Wandering when lost is the single biggest cause of survival situations becoming fatal. There is a simple acronym used widely by search-and-rescue and adopted by the NPS and USFS: **S.T.O.P.**

## S — Stop
The instant you suspect you are lost, stop moving. Find a safe spot — out of weather, away from cliffs or rivers. Sit down. Drink some water. Eat a snack if you have one. The first goal is to calm your physiology so you can think clearly.

## T — Think
Where did you last know where you were? When was that? How long have you been walking since? In what direction? What landmarks have you passed? What features of the terrain do you see now (sun position, water flow direction, distant peaks)? Get out your map and compare what you see to the map. Most "lost" situations are actually "temporarily off route" — usually a wrong turn within the last hour.

## O — Observe
Look up, listen, take stock. Check the sun for general direction. Listen for water, traffic, or voices. Get out your map and compass; orient the map. If you have a phone, even with no signal it usually still has a GPS chip — open a map app that supports offline maps and check your position. (Many phones can give a GPS fix without cell service.) If you're carrying a PLB or satellite communicator, you don't have to wait to use it. Activating early in daylight is safer than waiting until dark.

## P — Plan
Decide on a plan and only then move. Options:
1. **Backtrack carefully** to the last point you knew, if the terrain allows safe return.
2. **Stay put** if it's late, weather is poor, or terrain is dangerous. People who stay put are found faster than people who wander. Make yourself visible: bright clothing on a branch, an "X" of rocks or branches in an open area visible from the air, a smoky fire (if safe).
3. **Move to a known feature** like a road, river, or ridge if you can identify one — but only if you can do so without descending into worse terrain.

## Signaling
- Three of anything is the universal distress signal: three whistle blasts, three fires in a triangle, three flashes.
- A whistle carries much further than a voice and uses much less energy. Carry one on a lanyard.
- A signal mirror flashes visible from miles away in clear weather; aim at the horizon and slowly sweep.
- If you hear a helicopter, get to open ground and signal with bright clothing waved overhead.

## Stay warm, stay hydrated
Most search-and-rescue fatalities are hypothermia, not injury. Insulate from the ground. Drink water. Eat if you have food.

Search teams find lost hikers faster than panicked hikers find themselves. Help them find you by staying calm, staying visible, and staying put.
